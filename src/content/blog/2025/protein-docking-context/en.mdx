Since the first murine monoclonal antibody OKT3 appeared in 1986, antibody therapeutics have advanced through humanization and expanded into monoclonal antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, bispecific antibodies, and other forms. They are now central therapeutic strategies in oncology, autoimmune disease, and other fields. As a result, accurate prediction of antibody-antigen complex conformations, the Ab-Ag docking problem, has become a core challenge.

This is especially true when multispecific antibody combinations are involved. Epitope selection and cell-level binding dynamics can produce unexpected outcomes. Beyond more conventional allosteric mechanisms, this essay discusses several more innovative strategies and asks how protein docking should be formulated in real applications.

Take CD47 antibodies as an example. Their simple blocking effect at the cellular level may lead to complex functional consequences. One relevant paper is titled:

> An anti-CD47 antibody binds to a distinct epitope in a novel metal ion-dependent manner to minimize cross-linking of red blood cells.

Because CD47 is expressed on both tumor cells and red blood cells, anti-CD47 antibodies may cause red-cell cross-linking, leading to hemagglutination and anemia. Early drugs such as magrolimab encountered this type of issue. One strategy is to use a 1+1 format PD-1/CD47 bispecific antibody. In general, however, the single-arm blocking effect of a 1+1 format bispecific may be weaker than that of a 2+2 format. Careful epitope selection may allow a 2+2 format to achieve a similar reduction in cross-linking.

The "on" phase of antibody binding usually depends on molecular thermal motion in specific orientations. Once one Fab arm binds, it may increase the binding rate constant of the other arm to another copy of the same protein on the same cell surface. This requires the antibody molecule to have enough conformational flexibility to achieve bivalent binding on the same cell. By fine-tuning the spatial orientation of the antigen epitope, one can greatly increase the probability that an antibody binds two identical antigens on the same cell.

In the case of CD47, the antigen and Fab arm form an angle of roughly 130 degrees, causing the two CD47 molecules bound by one antibody to align almost in parallel with a narrow distance between them. This special spatial conformation prevents the antibody from cross-linking two different cells, thereby avoiding hemagglutination.

## Biparatopic Antibodies and Avidity

The major advantage of many biparatopic antibodies comes from a similar mechanism. By increasing avidity while maintaining relatively low affinity, they can achieve a high-avidity, low-affinity effect and improve targeting selectivity.

This strategy is especially effective for tumor-cell-surface antigens that contain multiple domains, such as CD22, mesothelin, and members of the CEACAM family. Because it is difficult in real antibody discovery to directly screen candidates with the desired avidity-affinity profile, combining binding units against different domains is often a practical route.

At a more refined molecular scale, functional differences from population-level binding dynamics become even more significant.

## SARS-CoV-2 and Bivalent Binding

The binding of SARS-CoV-2 spike and antibodies such as S309 provides a useful example:

> Cryo-ET of IgG bivalent binding on SARS-CoV-2 provides structural basis for antibody avidity.

Here, the binding question is not just whether an antibody can dock to an antigen. It is whether the geometry of the antigen arrangement, antibody flexibility, epitope accessibility, and spatial organization allow bivalent binding under realistic biological conditions.

## What Context Should Docking Models Capture?

In practical drug discovery, docking should be formulated according to the actual decision being made. Sometimes the question is whether two proteins can bind at all. Sometimes it is which epitope should be targeted. Sometimes it is whether an antibody format will create cross-linking risk. Sometimes it is whether the predicted conformation supports the desired cell-level function.

The model formulation should follow the biological question.

For AI tools such as Chai-1 and other molecular-interaction models, the value lies not only in predicting a complex structure. It lies in helping researchers reason about which structural possibilities matter. A model that outputs several plausible conformations without connecting them to function may still leave the experimental team uncertain.

## Conclusion

Finding the context of protein docking means asking what the docking result is for. In antibody discovery, geometry, avidity, epitope, format, flexibility, and cell-level behavior all matter. A useful docking model should not only answer "what does it look like?" It should help answer "what should we do next?"

That is the difference between structural prediction as a picture and structural prediction as a drug-discovery decision tool.
