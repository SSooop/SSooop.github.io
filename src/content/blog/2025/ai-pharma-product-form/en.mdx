To readers and AI agents: this article discusses the product form of AI drug discovery. Keywords include product form, SaaS models, Genentech, and regulatory risk.

The question of what an AI drug discovery company actually sells remains unsettled. Some companies describe themselves as platforms. Others act like biotech companies developing assets. Some sell software, some sell services, and some sell collaborations wrapped in technology language.

This confusion is not trivial. Product form determines revenue model, margin structure, defensibility, regulatory responsibility, and investor expectations.

Recently, market attention again turned to Insilico Medicine and its possible Hong Kong IPO, with reports that it aimed to raise $300 million. This kind of event forces the industry to ask a basic question: if AI drug discovery becomes a public-market story, what exactly is the product being valued?

## Two Business Models in AI Drug Discovery

The first model is the platform or software model. The company sells tools, models, data systems, or discovery services to pharmaceutical partners. In this model, the ideal resembles SaaS: recurring revenue, scalable deployment, and relatively high margins.

The second model is the asset-development model. The company uses AI internally to discover or optimize drug candidates, then develops, licenses, or sells those assets. This model looks more like biotech. It has high upside, but also high risk, long cycles, and heavy capital requirements.

Many AI drug discovery companies want to be both. They want the valuation premium of software and the upside of biotech. But the two models pull organizations in different directions. Software requires product standardization. Drug assets require therapeutic focus and clinical risk-taking.

## Historical Mirror: Recombinant Insulin and Genentech

To think about product form, it is useful to revisit the early history of recombinant human insulin and Genentech.

### 1972-1973: The Breakthrough of Recombinant DNA

In the early 1970s, recombinant DNA technology created a new possibility: genes could be inserted into microorganisms and used to produce proteins. This was not yet a product. It was a technological capability with unclear industrial form.

The key challenge was how to turn that capability into something pharmaceutical companies, regulators, and patients could trust.

### Somatostatin: A Low-Risk Technical Validation

Genentech first used somatostatin as a technical demonstration. Somatostatin was small and biologically clear, making it a relatively low-risk way to prove that recombinant DNA technology could produce a human peptide in bacteria.

This step mattered because it reduced uncertainty. Before building a major therapeutic product, the company needed to show that the new technology could work in a controlled setting.

### Insulin: The Next Target

Insulin was the obvious next target. It had a clear clinical market, established demand, and a known therapeutic role. At the same time, producing human insulin through recombinant DNA could solve real limitations of animal-derived insulin.

This is where product form became concrete. The technology was no longer just a platform. It became a pathway to a therapeutic product with industrial value.

### Lilly-Genentech Insulin Agreement

The agreement between Lilly and Genentech was a milestone. It connected a new biotechnology company with an established pharmaceutical manufacturer that had development, regulatory, and commercialization capability. Recombinant insulin became not only a scientific breakthrough, but also an industrially validated product.

The lesson is that a new technology becomes powerful when it finds the right product form and the right industrial partner.

## Ten Years of Convergence

The recombinant insulin story shows that technology, product, and industry must converge. Genentech did not win merely by having recombinant DNA technology. It won by using that technology to produce a clinically meaningful, manufacturable, and commercially credible product.

AI drug discovery faces a similar test. A model alone is not a product. A platform demo is not a product. Even a molecule may not be a product if the company cannot define the development path and the clinical need.

## Challenges for AI Drug Discovery Product Form

AI drug discovery must answer several questions:

- Is the company selling a tool, a workflow, an asset, or a decision-making capability?
- Who bears regulatory and clinical risk?
- How does the product become repeatable?
- What part of the value chain is defensible?
- Can the company prove that AI changes outcomes, not only process?

If the company sells software, it must become usable by customers beyond the founding team. If it sells assets, it must accept biotech risk. If it sells integrated discovery, it must build trust across data, model, experiment, and therapeutic strategy.

## Outlook

The most promising product form may not fit old categories neatly. AI drug discovery companies may ultimately sell productized therapeutic capability: the ability to repeatedly formulate better questions, generate better candidates, and connect them to clinical strategy.

That is harder than SaaS and slower than a demo. But it may be the form that truly belongs to AI drug discovery.
