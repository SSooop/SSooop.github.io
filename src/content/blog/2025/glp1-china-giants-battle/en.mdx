On August 7, 2025, Eli Lilly released Phase 3 data for the oral weight-loss drug
orforglipron. Participants lost an average of 12.4% of their body weight after 72 weeks,
slightly below Wall Street's 15% expectation, and Lilly shares fell more than 10% in
pre-market trading. The reaction corrected part of the rally that followed the June 21
data release—and showed just how harshly investors were judging every development in the
GLP-1 market.

The 2025 American Diabetes Association (ADA) meeting had already laid out the contrasting,
multi-layered strategies of Lilly and Novo Nordisk:

- On June 14, ahead of the meeting, Lilly reported Phase 1 data for the amylin analogue
  eloralintide. At the highest dose, participants lost an average of 11.3% of their body
  weight after 12 weeks, with encouraging gastrointestinal tolerability. The market
  nevertheless remained cautious, and Lilly's shares pulled back the following week.

- On June 21, Novo Nordisk presented Phase 3b STEP UP data for a higher, 7.2 mg dose of
  Wegovy (semaglutide), which delivered 21% weight loss. The market response was muted.

- Also on June 21, Lilly released Phase 3 data for oral orforglipron. At week 40, the
  high-dose group had lost an average of 7.3 kg (about 16 lb), or 7.9% of baseline body
  weight—slightly ahead of expectations.

- On June 22, Novo Nordisk reported results from the Phase 3 REDEFINE 1 and 2 trials of
  CagriSema, its fixed-dose combination of semaglutide and the amylin analogue cagrilintide.
  Efficacy was strong, but gastrointestinal adverse events were substantial and the overall
  results fell somewhat short of expectations.

- On June 23, Lilly reported that combining the ActRIIA/B antibody bimagrumab with Wegovy
  for 48 weeks produced an average weight reduction of 22.1%, with fat accounting for 92.8%
  of the weight lost. The results materially exceeded expectations and highlighted the
  clinical importance of preserving lean mass during obesity treatment.

From an industry perspective, Novo Nordisk is playing a largely defensive game. Investor
concern about Lilly is directed less at product efficacy than at the near-term returns from
its diversified portfolio. The market may also be overstating the total opportunity in
obesity treatment. Responses vary substantially between patients, while gastrointestinal
and psychiatric adverse events can undermine adherence. Those constraints make the most
optimistic forecasts difficult to take at face value.

Lilly, in fact, began building a layered offensive strategy early. Orforglipron is designed
to broaden access through the convenience and potentially lower cost of an oral therapy.
Retatrutide, meanwhile, activates GIP, GLP-1, and glucagon receptors to combine appetite
suppression, insulin secretion, and greater energy expenditure. At the highest dose,
retatrutide produced average weight loss of 24.2%—approaching the effect of bariatric
surgery.

At that level, simply pushing efficacy higher has diminishing clinical value. The more
important R&D challenge is to solve the limitations of today's therapies. Andrew Pannu,
founder of Sleuth Insights, framed the opportunity this way:

Another priority is to prepare for a post-GLP-1 market built around tightly defined
indications.

The strategic logic can be summarized in two parts:

> **Complication-specific indications:** Build treatments around obesity-related conditions
> such as MASH, chronic kidney disease, and osteoarthritis. Distinct clinical endpoints and
> reimbursement pathways can differentiate these therapies from broad weight-loss drugs.
>
> **Patients underserved by GLP-1 therapy:** Develop second-line options for people who do
> not respond adequately, regain weight, or cannot tolerate gastrointestinal adverse events.
> This is a substantial gap: roughly half of patients discontinue treatment within a year.

On August 15, the FDA approved semaglutide for adults with MASH and moderate-to-advanced
fibrosis (F2-F3), effectively validating the first of these strategies.

In China, the current red-ocean competition partly repeats the race to the bottom seen in
PD-1, but it also creates new strategic openings. The prevalence of diabetes and obesity is
not in doubt. The practical question is whether companies will remain followers pursuing
me-too products. The current pipelines reveal several alternative strategies:

frequent dosing can compensate for weaker headline weight-loss efficacy—and whether that
trade-off creates clinically meaningful differentiation.

Among multi-target programs, obesity-related complications—especially
metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)—are a major focus. Several Chinese
companies have advanced GIPR/GCGR dual- or triple-agonist programs toward clinical proof of
concept, including RAY-1225 from Guangdong Raynovent and ZX2021 from Tianjin Zhongxin.

Other MASH pipelines incorporate an FGF21 mechanism, building on evidence first reported
for the FGF21 analogue pegbelfermin in 2018: the pathway can regulate hepatic lipid
metabolism and may reduce fibrosis. Chinese programs include HEC88473, MWN-101 and MWN-109
from Minwei Bio, and DR10624 from Huadong Medicine. Data presented for DR10624 on May 12
showed relative reductions in liver-fat content of 51.9%, 77.8%, 79.0%, and 75.8% across
the 12.5, 25, 50, and 75 mg dose groups, respectively.

Safety, however, remains the central constraint on any multi-target strategy. Designing
around comorbidities from the outset—after careful safety characterization—can sharpen
target selection and improve commercial potential.

GLP-1 receptor agonists drive weight loss primarily through appetite suppression in the
central nervous system. Weight regain after discontinuation can be especially pronounced in
people who do not meet formal diagnostic criteria for obesity. Beyond combinations with
ActRIIA/B antibodies, metabolic pathways such as thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ) may
therefore offer another way to extend clinical benefit. Programs in development include
ASC47 and Hinova Pharmaceuticals' HP515.

For ActRIIA/B programs, the binding profiles of Laekna's LAE123 and bimagrumab deserve close
attention. Animal studies suggest that dual inhibition of ActRIIA and ActRIIB produces
greater muscle growth than targeting either receptor alone. It remains unclear, however,
whether differences in relative affinity for the two receptors translate into meaningful
biological differences.

Bimagrumab has reported dissociation constants of 1.7 pM for ActRIIB and 434 pM for
ActRIIA, while CDD861—the antibody described in the cited paper—has values of 62 pM and
134 pM, respectively. In practical terms, their downstream signal-inhibition profiles do
not appear markedly different. The paper nevertheless highlights an important advantage of
targeting the receptors rather than individual ligands:

> If one were to achieve this same effect with an antibody to the ligands, it would be necessary to simultaneously inhibit myostatin, activins, and perhaps GDF11 as well, in those conditions in which it is elevated.
> https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1707925114

For that reason, I remain skeptical of combinations built around ligand-neutralizing
antibodies.

Finally, the presumed convenience advantage of oral small molecules deserves closer
scrutiny. For GLP-1 therapies, adherence may be shaped more by gastrointestinal adverse
events than by the route of administration itself. Ascletis Pharma's decision to develop
both oral and subcutaneous formulations of ASC30 reflects that uncertainty.

On August 19, Viking Therapeutics reported topline Phase 2 VENTURE-Oral data for the oral
GLP-1/GIP dual agonist VK2735. Discontinuation was substantially higher in the treatment
arms than in the placebo arm, and Viking's shares fell 42%. At the same time, prefilled pens
have made long-acting injectables such as semaglutide easy to self-administer. The choice
between a pill and an injection is therefore a question of real-world clinical performance,
not an automatic advantage for oral delivery. Daily tablets can be missed, and oral
formulations may still carry substantial gastrointestinal burdens.

On August 18, Novo Nordisk announced that self-paying patients could obtain Ozempic through
NovoCare for $499 per month—roughly half the prevailing U.S. list price. As obesity drugs
move toward mass-market use, competition is expanding beyond molecules and clinical data
into distribution. NovoCare and LillyDirect are becoming extensions of the rivalry between
Novo Nordisk and Lilly.

Commercial execution matters. But the broader adoption of obesity medicines should
ultimately serve their original purpose: improving metabolic health.
