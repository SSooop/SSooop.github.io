When we turn our attention to vertical AI applications, we often find ourselves circling back to AI drug discovery. This almost echoes Xin Qiji's famous line from "The Lantern Festival": after searching a thousand times in the crowd, one suddenly turns back and finds the person standing where the lights are dim.

Many AI drug discovery startups today choose "undruggable" targets as a proof of technological strength. That choice itself has a flavor of "searching a thousand times." From an industry-observation perspective, companies are eager to demonstrate technical breakthroughs around difficult targets, yet they often avoid some of the pharmaceutical industry's more fundamental questions. This essay starts with the historical origin of undruggability, then uses classic breast-cancer targets such as BRCA1 and TP53 to analyze why certain targets are difficult to drug. It then turns to the new-pocket case of the KRAS-G12C inhibitor sotorasib and discusses whether AI is truly useful in finding new druggable sites. Finally, it returns to the basic logic of the pharmaceutical industry and asks what kind of innovation AI should really pursue.

The term "undruggable target" has a long history and is closely connected to the development of modern drug discovery. In 2002, Hopkins and Groom published "The Druggable Genome" in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, giving one of the first systematic formulations of this concept. Importantly, the concept originally emerged from the perspective of small-molecule drug discovery.

After decades of small-molecule research, the main obstacles behind undruggability can be summarized as follows. Some proteins lack a stable binding pocket. Some are located in difficult cellular compartments. Some participate in broad protein-protein interaction networks where selective intervention is hard. Some targets are biologically important but structurally flexible, lowly expressed, or functionally redundant. The label "undruggable" therefore does not mean that biology is irrelevant; it means that the existing technical paradigm cannot easily convert the target into a useful therapeutic intervention.

Breast cancer offers a useful example. BRCA1 and TP53 are both deeply involved in tumor biology, yet they are not straightforward small-molecule targets. BRCA1 is a tumor suppressor involved in DNA repair. Loss of function is the disease driver, so simply inhibiting the protein is not a therapeutic answer. TP53 is similar. It is one of the most important tumor suppressors in cancer biology, but mutations, loss of function, and context-dependent effects make it extremely hard to modulate through a classical small-molecule strategy.

This is why the history of cancer therapy often moves indirectly. Instead of directly drugging BRCA1, the industry developed PARP inhibitors based on synthetic lethality. Instead of directly "fixing" TP53 in all contexts, researchers have explored downstream pathways, mutation-specific stabilizers, immunotherapy strategies, and gene-therapy ideas. The core lesson is that a target's biological importance and its druggability are not the same thing.

For AI drug discovery, this distinction is crucial. If AI is used only to search for a pocket on a famous target, it may still remain trapped inside the old small-molecule imagination. The more important question is whether AI can help reframe the therapeutic strategy itself.

KRAS was long considered an undruggable target. Its surface looked smooth, its binding affinity for GTP/GDP was high, and its biology was central to cancer. Yet the discovery of covalent inhibitors against KRAS-G12C changed the industry's imagination. Sotorasib showed that an apparently undruggable target could become tractable when researchers found a mutation-specific pocket and a chemically exploitable mechanism.

This case is often used to justify the current wave of AI pocket discovery. That argument is partly right. AI may help researchers explore conformational ensembles, identify transient pockets, and generate molecules for difficult binding sites. But the KRAS-G12C story is not only a story about finding a pocket. It is a story about aligning mutation biology, chemical reactivity, patient selection, and clinical development.

The pocket mattered because it was embedded in a complete therapeutic logic.

AI should not be reduced to a pocket-finding machine. Better models, molecular docking, and generative chemistry are all useful, but they are not the whole value chain of drug discovery. If AI only helps a company make a slightly better ligand for a fashionable difficult target, the result may still be a narrow technology demo.

The more interesting possibility is therapy discovery. AI can help integrate target biology, disease mechanism, patient stratification, modality choice, safety constraints, biomarker strategy, and clinical endpoint design. This means moving from "Can we bind this target?" to "What intervention should exist for this disease state?"

The distinction matters because drug discovery is not a target contest. It is a clinical problem-solving process. A target is only meaningful when it becomes part of a therapy that changes patient outcomes.

The current enthusiasm for undruggable targets sometimes hides an uncomfortable fact: not every difficult target is worth solving. Some targets are scientifically attractive but clinically weak. Others are clinically important but require a different therapeutic modality rather than a better small molecule. If AI drug discovery simply chases difficulty, it may end up confusing technical achievement with medical value.

The "place where the lights are dim" may not be the most famous undruggable target. It may be the neglected clinical question whose therapeutic logic has not yet been correctly formulated.

The future of AI drug discovery should be judged by whether it expands the industry's ability to define therapies. The target is a coordinate. The pocket is a handle. The molecule is a tool. The therapy is the real product.

If AI can help us discover new therapeutic mechanisms, understand which patient population truly benefits, and design interventions that traditional discovery would not have imagined, then AI will deserve its revolutionary language. Otherwise, it will remain another instrument in the long history of pharmaceutical optimization.

The task, then, is not to prove that AI can find a pocket. The task is to prove that AI can help medicine find its next real answer.
