In the first half of 2025, Novo Nordisk's (NYSE: NVO) semaglutide franchise generated
DKK 112.756 billion—approximately $16.7 billion—in sales, making it the world's
highest-grossing pharmaceutical franchise. Yet the company's share price remained roughly 50%
below its second-quarter 2024 peak.

Novo responded to intensifying competition on several fronts. It launched the REDEFINE 11 trial
to evaluate CagriSema, a combination of semaglutide and the amylin analogue cagrilintide; it
submitted a European marketing application for a higher 7.2 mg dose of Wegovy; and it changed
leadership, with Maziar Mike Doustdar succeeding Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen as president and CEO
on August 7. Behind all three moves was the same pressure: Eli Lilly's (NYSE: LLY) rapidly
growing tirzepatide franchise.

The roots of the rivalry reach back to insulin in the 1920s. In 1923, Danish Nobel laureate
August Krogh, motivated by his wife Marie's diabetes, established Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium
with Hans Christian Hagedorn. Two years later, former colleagues founded Novo Terapeutisk
Laboratorium, beginning more than six decades of competition in Danish insulin. The companies
merged in 1989 to form Novo Nordisk.

Eli Lilly followed a different path. Founded in 1876, it introduced Iletin—the first
commercially available insulin product—in 1923 and quickly established a leading position in
the U.S. diabetes market.

The scientific story began with a curious observation. In 1964, two independent teams found
that oral glucose stimulated substantially more insulin secretion than the same glucose
delivered intravenously. The “incretin effect” was the first strong evidence that the gut
actively regulates insulin through secreted signals.

Researchers identified glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), a cleavage product of proglucagon in
intestinal tissue, in 1986. In 1992, John Eng's team isolated exendin-4 from Gila monster venom.
It resembled GLP-1 but remained metabolically active far longer, making a practical receptor
agonist conceivable. Other work showed that GLP-1 also promotes satiety, suppresses appetite,
and delays gastric emptying. The molecule was not merely a glucose-lowering signal; it had the
potential to become an obesity therapy.

Obesity drug development carried a recent trauma. Fen-Phen, the combination of fenfluramine and
phentermine, reached 18 million U.S. prescriptions in 1996 and was widely treated as a
breakthrough. In 1997, however, Mayo Clinic clinicians reported heart-valve abnormalities and
pulmonary hypertension in 24 patients. The death of Mary Linnen intensified public concern.
After the FDA confirmed the association with cardiac harm, fenfluramine was withdrawn that
September. The episode made the industry wary of obesity drugs even as the therapeutic promise
of GLP-1 was becoming clearer.

GLP-1 receptor agonists also faced a drug-engineering bottleneck. Natural GLP-1 has a
half-life of only a few minutes, making it impractical as a therapy. The only known
long-acting compound, exendin-4, was controlled by Amylin Pharmaceuticals after it acquired
rights from John Eng's team, and safety concerns around an animal-venom peptide kept many
companies away. Amid regulatory penalties for contamination at a Novo Nordisk manufacturing
site, the company reorganized and put 30-year-old researcher Lotte Bjerre Knudsen in charge
of its GLP-1 program. Her team developed liraglutide in 1996, using molecular modification
to extend its half-life to roughly 12 hours. Eli Lilly, meanwhile, had enough cash flow from
Zyprexa and Prozac to absorb failure in GLP-1. Even when its early analogue LY315902 missed
clinical expectations, the company could maintain strategic continuity.

Amylin Pharmaceuticals licensed rights to exendin-4 and filed an investigational new drug
application in 1999, but limited funding slowed development. In 2002, Lilly committed $325
million through a joint-development agreement. Lilly's capital and commercial infrastructure
accelerated the program, and the FDA approved Byetta in April 2005 as the world's first GLP-1
receptor agonist for type 2 diabetes.

Novo's liraglutide followed a slower path from diabetes to obesity. Approved as Victoza for type
2 diabetes in 2009, it was not initially developed around weight management even though Lotte
Bjerre Knudsen had recognized its effect on body weight. Her continued advocacy helped move the
obesity program forward. In 2014, a higher dose was approved as Saxenda, the first GLP-1 therapy
specifically authorized for obesity. Before the category became crowded, liraglutide established
an early lead with a more attractive safety profile than older agents such as orlistat and
Contrave.

Novo built on its first-mover advantage with semaglutide, launched for diabetes in 2017, and
Wegovy, approved for chronic weight management in June 2021. Lilly responded through
differentiation rather than imitation. Its partnership with Amylin had ended in 2011 after a
dispute over Lilly's work with Boehringer Ingelheim on linagliptin. The separation accelerated
Lilly's internal incretin research and its shift toward tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor
agonist.

Wegovy's efficacy created extraordinary demand and helped drive Novo's valuation higher. But
the company did not expand manufacturing capacity quickly enough. Global shortages gave
competitors an opening that clinical performance alone could not close.

Persistent shortages placed Wegovy on the FDA drug-shortage list and allowed qualifying
compounding pharmacies to produce copies under shortage-related rules. The commercial
consequences became visible in July 2025, when Novo cut its full-year sales-growth forecast from
13–21% to 8–14% and its operating-profit growth forecast from 16–24% to 10–16%. The shares fell
21.83% that day—the largest single-day decline in the company's public-market history. Investors
were questioning both management execution and the competitive threat from tirzepatide.

The FDA approved Lilly's tirzepatide brand Zepbound for chronic weight management in November
2023, and head-to-head data later showed greater weight loss than Wegovy. Lilly also invested
aggressively in capacity before and after launch, avoiding shortages on Novo's scale. In the
first half of 2025, Zepbound generated $5.693 billion in sales, up 223% year over year and
essentially level with Wegovy's DKK 36.89 billion—about $5.68 billion—over the same period.
Lilly had converted a differentiated mechanism and stronger execution into a second-generation
incretin advantage.

Novo's disadvantage did not come from weak science. It came from strategic caution and a supply
chain that could not respond to the demand its own product created. Tirzepatide used the
clinical advantage of dual agonism to capture the premium self-pay market, while Lilly combined
clinical value, payment convenience, and manufacturing responsiveness. Howard Marks's
observation captures the cost of timing:

> Being right too early is not much different from being wrong—and, of course, being right too
> late is even less valuable.

The GLP-1 war is far from over. Scientific leadership matters, but the next phase will also be
decided by manufacturing, access, and the ability to act before an advantage becomes a
constraint.
