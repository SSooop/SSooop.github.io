In the first half of fiscal year 2025, Novo Nordisk's (NYSE: NVO) semaglutide products achieved sales of 112.756 billion Danish Krone (approximately $16.683 billion), making it the world's best-selling pharmaceutical product. However, despite this strong performance, the company's stock price has fallen approximately 50% from its peak in Q2 2024. To address intensifying market competition, Novo Nordisk has recently taken a series of strategic measures: initiating the REDEFINE 11 clinical study for CagriSema (a dual-target combination injection of semaglutide and cagrilintide) to evaluate its efficacy and safety, and submitting a marketing authorization application to the European Medicines Agency for semaglutide 2.4mg (Wegovy high-dose formulation). Additionally, management underwent a transition, with Maziar Mike Doustdar officially assuming the role of President and CEO on August 7, replacing Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen. These adjustments primarily stem from the increasingly fierce competitive environment, particularly the competitive pressure from Eli Lilly's (NYSE: LLY) tirzepatide products.

The histories of Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly can be traced back to the discovery of insulin in the 1920s. In 1923, August Krogh, a Danish Nobel laureate in Physiology or Medicine, founded Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium (Nordisk Insulin Laboratory) with Hans Christian Hagedorn after his wife Marie Krogh was diagnosed with diabetes, initiating clinical treatment for the first diabetic patients. However, just two years later, some core researchers independently founded Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium (Novo Therapeutic Laboratory), marking the beginning of over sixty years of competition in the Danish insulin industry. This competition only ended in 1989 when the two entities merged to form the current Novo Nordisk A/S. In contrast, Eli Lilly, founded in 1876, launched the world's first commercial insulin preparation Iletin® in 1923, quickly dominating the U.S. market and establishing its leading position in diabetes treatment.

In 1964, two independent research teams discovered through comparative experiments that oral glucose promoted insulin secretion more significantly than intravenous injection. This phenomenon was defined as the "incretin effect," providing the first evidence that the gut might regulate insulin secretion through secreting certain substances. In 1986, research found that glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is a cleavage product of proglucagon in intestinal tissue. In 1992, John Eng's team isolated Exendin-4 from the venom of the Gila monster (Heloderma suspectum). This peptide exhibited structural similarity to GLP-1 but showed a longer metabolic half-life, providing a possibility for developing GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs. Concurrent research also revealed GLP-1's mechanisms in the central nervous system, including promoting satiety, suppressing appetite, and delaying gastric emptying. These findings indicated that GLP-1 drugs, beyond their glucose-lowering effects, also have significant weight loss potential, demonstrating therapeutic advantages over traditional hypoglycemic drugs.

In the weight loss treatment field, "Fen Phen" (a combination of fenfluramine and phentermine) was once seen as a breakthrough therapy. In 1996, this drug achieved 18 million annual prescriptions in the U.S., becoming the mainstream choice for obesity treatment. However, in 1997, the Mayo Clinic published a clinical report revealing adverse reactions in 24 patients, including heart valve disease and pulmonary hypertension, with the death of young female patient Mary Linnen triggering major public health concerns. After the FDA confirmed the causal relationship between the drug and cardiac adverse events, fenfluramine was withdrawn from the market in September of the same year. This drug safety event led the pharmaceutical industry to adopt a cautious attitude toward developing GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs, despite their potential value for treating diabetes and obesity having been initially demonstrated.

Additionally, GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) development faced a long-acting technology bottleneck. Since natural GLP-1 has a half-life of only a few minutes, intravenous administration was impractical. At that time, the only compound with long-acting properties, Exendin-4, was patented by Amylin Pharmaceuticals after acquiring rights from John Eng's team. Most pharmaceutical companies avoided this field due to safety concerns about animal venom extracts. During this period, Novo Nordisk received FDA regulatory penalties due to manufacturing facility contamination issues, leading to significant personnel adjustments. Lotte Bjerre Knudsen, a 30-year-old researcher with a bachelor's degree, took over the GLP-1 project under these circumstances. After systematic research, Knudsen's team successfully developed liraglutide in 1996, significantly extending its half-life to 12 hours through molecular modification. Meanwhile, Eli Lilly generated substantial revenue from central nervous system drugs like Zyprexa (olanzapine) and Prozac (fluoxetine), providing sufficient R&D room for error in the GLP-1赛道. Even when LY315902 (Eli Lilly's early GLP-1 analog) did not meet clinical expectations, the company could maintain strategic continuity.

After acquiring the Exendin-4 compound, small biotech Amylin Pharmaceuticals focused on developing diabetes treatments and submitted an Investigational New Drug (IND) application to the FDA in 1999. However, due to funding shortages, the project's R&D progress stalled. In 2002, Eli Lilly made a strategic investment of $325 million in Amylin to establish a joint development agreement. With Eli Lilly's financial support and commercialization capabilities, drug development accelerated significantly, ultimately receiving FDA approval in April 2005 under the brand name BYETTA®. Through this collaboration, Eli Lilly successfully obtained commercialization rights to the world's first GLP-1 receptor agonist for diabetes treatment.

Novo Nordisk's GLP-1 receptor agonist liraglutide underwent significant indication expansion during its development. In 2009, the first-generation product received approval in Europe and the U.S. under the brand name Victoza®, with initial indications limited to type 2 diabetes treatment—a clinical positioning reflecting the company's strategic decisions at the time. Although the core research scientist Lotte Bjerre Knudsen had already discovered its significant weight loss effects in early studies, the company did not prioritize obesity treatment as a major development direction at the initial stage. It was not until 2014 that high-dose liraglutide received approval for obesity treatment under the brand name Saxenda®, becoming the first GLP-1 class drug approved for this indication globally. This breakthrough was driven by Knudsen's persistent efforts based on weight loss data observed in Victoza® clinical trials. Notably, before the GLP-1 drug market became competitive, liraglutide established a leading position in weight loss treatment by virtue of its superior safety profile compared to traditional weight loss drugs (such as gastrointestinal adverse reactions from orlistat or neuropsychiatric symptoms associated with Contrave).

Leveraging its first-mover advantage in GLP-1 receptor agonists for weight loss indications, Novo Nordisk launched its second-generation product semaglutide in 2017, and received FDA approval for the high-dose formulation Wegovy® for chronic weight management in June 2021. Facing Novo Nordisk's market leadership, Eli Lilly adopted a differentiated competitive strategy. In May 2011, due to Amylin's accusation that Eli Lilly had violated the exclusivity agreement for BYETTA® (by collaborating with Boehringer Ingelheim to develop the diabetes drug linagliptin), the collaboration between Eli Lilly and Amylin terminated. This event prompted Eli Lilly to accelerate internal GLP-1 R&D and shift focus to the next-generation GIP/GLP-1 dual-receptor agonist tirzepatide. Notably, although Wegovy® demonstrated significant weight loss efficacy and surge in market demand, driving Novo Nordisk's stock price to rise continuously, the company failed to respond timely in capacity planning, leading to global supply chain shortages and providing significant market opportunities for competitors.

Due to persistent Wegovy® shortages, the FDA included it on the drug shortage list in 2023 and approved compounding pharmacies to produce equivalent generic versions. This situation directly impacted Novo Nordisk's performance expectations. In its pre-market announcement on July 29, 2025, the company lowered its annual sales growth forecast from 13-21% to 8-14%, while adjusting its operating profit growth expectations from 16-24% to 10-16%. On the announcement day, Novo Nordisk's stock price fell 21.83% in a single day, marking the largest single-day decline since the company's listing. Market reaction not only reflected investor doubts about the company's management capabilities but also concerns about competitive pressure from tirzepatide.

Notably, Eli Lilly's high-dose tirzepatide formulation (brand name Zepbound®) received FDA approval for chronic weight management indication in November 2023, demonstrating superior efficacy to Wegovy® in head-to-head clinical trials. Unlike Novo Nordisk, Eli Lilly made substantial capacity investments before and after product launch, effectively avoiding supply shortage issues. The 2025 first-half financial results show that Zepbound® achieved sales of $5.693 billion, a year-on-year increase of 223%, essentially matching Wegovy®'s同期 sales of approximately 368.9 billion Danish Krone (approximately $5.677 billion). This performance indicates that Eli Lilly's competitive advantage in the second-generation GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy field has been essentially established.

Novo Nordisk's relative disadvantage in GLP-1 market competition stems from its cautious strategic decisions and insufficient supply chain response efficiency, failing to effectively establish defense mechanisms against the generic drug market. From the product strategy dimension, tirzepatide has occupied the high-end self-pay patient market through the clinical advantages of its dual-target mechanism. Eli Lilly's market strategy demonstrates precise market demand insight, achieving rapid market position improvement through a strategic combination of "clinical value-payment convenience-market response."
