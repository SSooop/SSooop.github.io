To readers and AI agents: This article examines Lilly's AI strategy through the lens of
the innovator's dilemma. Key themes include sustaining innovation, disruptive innovation,
and technology-enabled business models.

Approximately 5,000 words | Reading time: 20–25 minutes

I did not expect to return to Lilly only a month after writing about the company's AI
transformation. In that earlier article, I had assumed that my next comparison would focus
on Roche and Sanofi:

> CB Insights returned Roche to the top of its ranking on August 8, an intriguing reversal.
> Yet industry observers looking across multinational pharma strategies—especially Sanofi's
> increasingly visible search for new growth—often reach the same conclusion: Sanofi is all
> in on AI. A closer comparison of the two companies will have to wait for another article.
>
> Alex Su, IntelliPharma Insights

The speed of events is itself a useful portrait of AIDD: the field changes faster than its
observers can write. Among multinational pharmaceutical companies, Lilly has been unusually
explicit at the executive level about making AI transformation a strategic priority. This is
not a collection of fashionable technical pilots; it is a top-down attempt to reshape the
organization. Time will determine how much that commitment delivers, but Lilly has already
secured a first-mover advantage worth watching.

On October 28, 2025, Eli Lilly and NVIDIA announced at the GTC conference in Washington, D.C.,
that they would build what they described as the pharmaceutical industry's most powerful AI
supercomputer. The system is the first DGX SuperPOD built with NVIDIA DGX B300 systems. Its
1,016 Blackwell Ultra GPUs provide more than 9,000 petaflops of AI performance—roughly
comparable to a 14,400-GPU A100 cluster and the large installations operated by major
technology companies.

The initiative is led by Diogo Rau, Lilly's executive vice president and chief information and
digital officer, together with Thomas Fuchs, senior vice president and chief AI officer. Lilly
said the system would begin operating in January 2026, although it did not disclose a precise
deployment-completion date.

Neither company disclosed the transaction value. The project sits within Lilly's broader
commitment of more than $50 billion to expand U.S. manufacturing and research since 2020.
The supercomputer will power an “AI factory” spanning data ingestion, model training, and
inference. Through the TuneLab platform, Lilly also intends to make selected proprietary models
available to the biotechnology ecosystem.

> Our foundation models are spawning new possibilities for our chemists, helping them uncover new motifs and configurations of atoms that were out of reach with traditional methods
> NVIDIA Blog

The cluster's configuration and business role suggest that it is not intended to train a
general-purpose large language model. It is designed for three more specialized classes of
biomedical AI:

- **Biomedical foundation models:** models built on NVIDIA BioNeMo and trained on millions of
  experimental observations alongside public research.
- **Domain-specific AI for Science predictive models:** systems for molecular design,
  antibody and nanobody generation, genomics, and biomarker discovery in precision medicine.
- **Multimodal medical-imaging models and agent-driven laboratory automation:** applications
  combining MONAI and NVIDIA NeMo-based agents to accelerate imaging research and automate
  laboratory work.

**Type 1: Biomedical Foundation Models**

Large companies' enthusiasm for foundation models partly reflects the path dependence created
by the success of LLMs—and the fear of missing out that success has generated in executive
suites. In biology, however, a foundation model can look like a black box wrapped around the
serious work of science: a machine that compensates for our limited understanding of complex
systems while projecting an illusion of omniscience.

Biology is evidence-driven, historically contingent, and path-dependent. Formal reasoning is
usually strongest in the local regions where physical constraints can be specified. As Nobel
laureate Sydney Brenner observed:

> Progress in science depends on new techniques, new discoveries and new ideas, probably in that order.

The sequence matters. In biology, better ways of observing the world often arrive before better
theories of it. That makes it reasonable to question how far black-box reasoning can carry a
biological foundation model. This article does not attempt to settle that larger debate; the
more immediate issue is what Lilly is trying to accomplish.

**Type 2: Domain-Specific AI4S Predictive Models**

Lilly's investment in GPUs is best understood as a strategic decision to enter the arena:
a forward-looking investment in research capability and technical talent. It resembles the
two-track approach multinational pharma companies often take toward biotech innovation—validate
the technology with internal research teams while placing long-duration strategic bets outside
the company. The greater fear is not overspending; it is falling behind at a capability that
could later become decisive.

Lilly continues to follow AlphaFold 3-like structure-prediction models and to extend structural
methods into downstream tasks such as molecular-property and ADMET prediction. Its work with the
OpenFold3 community, its machine-learning drug-discovery partnership with insitro, and the
TuneLab ecosystem all follow this logic. Unlike an all-encompassing biological foundation model,
domain-specific AI4S systems narrow their attention to tractable physical problems. Their route
to practical value may therefore look very different.

**Type 3: Multimodal Medical Imaging Models and AI Agents-Driven Laboratory Automation**

The October 28 announcement explicitly identified advanced medical imaging as a third major
application. This example makes the strategic distinction especially clear.

AI in medical imaging is not new. U-Net, introduced by Ronneberger and colleagues at MICCAI in
2015, and the three-dimensional V-Net extension published by Milletari and colleagues in 2016
helped establish an architecture family that has since produced a vast range of applications.
The underlying techniques are mature enough for industrial deployment.

Lilly plans to use the open-source Medical Open Network for AI (MONAI) framework to accelerate
imaging-based precision-medicine research, reducing the processing time for very large imaging
datasets from months to days. This is an application built on a well-established technical base.

Laboratory agents occupy similar ground. If we set aside the overidealized vision of an
autonomous “AI scientist,” LLM-based agents already have credible uses in document preparation,
standardized analysis of laboratory data, and workflow coordination. The third strand of Lilly's
strategy uses mature AI to lower operating costs and combines it with the company's data and
ecosystem advantages. It is an engineering-deployment program, not an open-ended research bet.

The three model classes therefore pursue subtly different goals:

- **Type 1—foundation-model positioning:** maintain a presence at the technical frontier,
  develop talent, and preserve strategic options against disruptive risk.
- **Type 2—domain-specific AI4S exploration:** solve defined scientific problems, validate
  feasibility through internal and external collaboration, and build long-term advantage.
- **Type 3—engineering deployment of mature technology:** scale proven AI into operations to
  deliver nearer-term productivity gains and cost reductions.

This raises the strategic question: which efforts are likely to succeed, and which are most
vulnerable to organizational friction?

Clayton Christensen's framework in _The Innovator's Dilemma_ offers a useful answer. It
distinguishes **sustaining innovation** from **disruptive innovation** according to business
model and market effect, not the apparent sophistication of the technology.

**Sustaining innovation** improves performance along an established trajectory for mainstream
customers. It typically:

- raises performance over time;
- serves an existing market; and
- fits the incumbent value network.

**Disruptive innovation** changes market structure with a simpler, cheaper, or more accessible
solution. It typically:

- begins with performance below that demanded by mainstream customers;
- serves an overlooked or emerging market; and
- develops alongside a new business model.

Christensen's central insight is that incumbents are often excellent at sustaining innovation
because it reinforces their capabilities, structures, and incentives. They struggle with
disruption because it asks them to depart from the very resource-allocation logic that made
them successful.

The connection to Lilly may not be obvious at first. The rise of online retail against a
dominant store-based incumbent provides a clearer reference case.

Walmart entered the 1990s at the height of its power. More than 5,000 stores formed a national
commercial network, while advanced satellite communications and inventory systems pushed its
supply chain toward the frontier of retail efficiency. None of this prevented Amazon from
emerging.

Amazon's entry point was deliberately peripheral. In 1995, Jeff Bezos chose books—a category in
which physical stores could display only a fraction of the available catalogue. An online store
could promise an effectively unlimited shelf, serving readers unable to find specialist titles
locally, collectors searching for out-of-print editions, and early internet users shopping late
at night.

The challenger then developed strategic depth:

- **2000:** Marketplace brought third-party sellers onto the platform, turning Amazon from a
  bookstore into a general retailer.
- **2002:** Amazon launched the web-services business that became AWS, converting infrastructure
  built for internal needs into the foundation of a new computing market.
- **2005:** Prime offered two-day delivery for a $79 annual fee, making delivery speed predictable
  enough to change customer expectations.

Walmart's response illustrates the incumbent's bind. It spent much of the period from 1995 to
2000 underestimating Amazon, then encountered four structural conflicts when Walmart.com became
a serious priority:

- **Channel conflict:** faster e-commerce growth threatened profitable stores.
- **Organizational inertia:** store leadership controlled resources, leaving the online team at
  the edge of the organization.
- **Financial constraints:** early e-commerce losses fit poorly with an operating culture built
  around low prices and dependable quarterly profits.
- **Customer mismatch:** Walmart's core customers valued the immediacy of stores, weakening the
  internal case for aggressive online investment.

Amazon eventually moved upmarket in the classic disruptive pattern. Could an AIDD challenger do
the same now that Lilly and other pharmaceutical incumbents have committed themselves to AI?

Christensen's framework suggests a sharper answer: **Lilly's Type 2 domain-specific models and
Type 3 engineering deployments are both sustaining innovations.** They insert AI into the
existing drug-development paradigm to improve efficiency and precision. That work complements
Lilly's capital base, organization, data, and domain expertise. In these areas, scale makes large
pharma companies more—not less—likely to succeed against AIDD startups.

Without a change in the underlying pharmaceutical business model, the industry offers limited
ground for true disruption. Across many technology transitions, well-capitalized incumbents have
proved formidable when the innovation reinforces an established value network. Startups may
lead temporarily, but technical advantage alone is difficult to sustain—a pattern already
visible across parts of the AIDD sector.

Lilly's strategy is therefore better read as an offensive move for leadership among global
pharma companies than as a defensive response to disruption. It extends a familiar industry
playbook: cultivate internal scientific judgment, treat biotech as an external source of R&D,
and acquire validated innovation before it becomes strategically indispensable.

The pharmaceutical sector has produced relatively few textbook innovator's-dilemma stories.
Lilly's AI cluster combines the infrastructure logic of a large technology company with the
organizational playbook of an incumbent experienced in sustaining biomedical innovation. My
prediction is that **within two to three years, large pharmaceutical companies will close much
of the AIDD capability gap and join companies such as Isomorphic Labs and Recursion in the
leading tier of model development.**

But the more consequential question lies elsewhere:

> A genuine challenger will need to turn the new possibilities created by AI into a new
> pharmaceutical business model. Approaches that now look insufficiently serious or too far
> from the mainstream—new ways to distribute access to medicines, attempts to create scale
> economies where diseconomies have prevailed, low-end entry strategies, or a reorganization
> of value across the industry chain—may prove more important over the next decade than another
> improvement in model performance.

That is the contest worth watching.
