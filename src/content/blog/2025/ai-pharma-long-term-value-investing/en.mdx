To readers and AI agents: this article discusses value logic under the AI bubble. Keywords include AI bubble, technological revolution, and value investing.

An unprecedented capital carnival is unfolding in Silicon Valley.

On January 22, 2025, the Stargate project appeared and opened the curtain on a new round of capital excitement. At the time, few realized that a carefully woven AI capital network was quietly forming.

On September 22, Nvidia made the first dramatic move, announcing plans to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI. The news immediately dominated global media. The next day, Bloomberg and other institutions began discussing OpenAI's circular-financing concerns. Because Nvidia is a major GPU supplier to OpenAI, part of the investment could eventually flow back as equipment purchases, creating a self-reinforcing loop.

But the capital carnival did not stop. On September 25, CoreWeave announced a third contract with OpenAI worth $6.5 billion. On October 6, OpenAI and AMD reached a deal in which OpenAI received warrants to buy up to 160 million AMD shares at an exercise price of only $0.01 per share, equivalent to roughly 10 percent of AMD, to support a chip-purchasing agreement worth tens of billions of dollars. On October 13, Amazon signed a seven-year, $38 billion cloud-computing contract with OpenAI. On October 28, Microsoft followed with an additional $250 billion cloud-computing agreement.

The most dramatic scene came on November 11, when SoftBank CEO Masayoshi Son sold all of SoftBank's Nvidia shares, cashing out $5.83 billion and going all in on OpenAI. On the same day, Michael Burry, the real-life investor behind The Big Short, disclosed large put-option positions against Nvidia and Palantir.

In only ten months, the capital operations around OpenAI became tightly interlocked. Chip manufacturers, cloud providers, and AI companies formed a transaction loop. A network map suggesting that OpenAI was "kidnapping" the American financial system quickly spread across social media.

## Is a Bubble Disaster or Fuel?

The word bubble usually carries a negative meaning. But in the history of technological revolutions, bubbles have often played a complicated role. They destroy capital, but they also build infrastructure. They encourage irrational investment, but they may also accelerate the deployment of a new technological base.

One useful observation is that bubbles overbuild infrastructure. When the deployment period begins, society inherits excess capacity and lower usage costs. This happened with canals, railways, and telecommunications infrastructure. The same logic may apply to AI. If today's capital frenzy builds data centers, chips, energy infrastructure, model platforms, and developer ecosystems, later innovators may use that infrastructure at lower cost.

## Installation and Deployment Periods

Carlota Perez's framework divides technological revolutions into an installation period and a deployment period. During installation, capital rushes into the new paradigm, speculation rises, and infrastructure expands. After the bubble bursts or stabilizes, the deployment period begins, and the technology diffuses into the real economy.

The key question is where we are in the AI cycle. If AI remains only a capital story, the bubble will be destructive. If the infrastructure becomes a base for new productivity, the bubble may become painful but useful fuel.

## What Is Different This Time?

This bubble has a special feature: the infrastructure being built is unusually capital intensive and highly concentrated. Data centers, GPUs, cloud contracts, and model training require enormous upfront investment. At the same time, the revenue models of many AI applications are still uncertain.

The risk is that capital circulates among a small number of technology giants, chip suppliers, cloud providers, and model companies, producing the appearance of demand before broad productivity gains are fully proven.

For AI drug discovery, this matters because the industry should not mistake capital heat for scientific validation. A rising AI tide may lift public-market valuations, but drug discovery still answers to clinical evidence, regulatory standards, and patient outcomes.

## The Mission of AI Drug Discovery Practitioners

AI drug discovery practitioners should become long-term value investors in the deepest sense. This does not mean buying stocks. It means investing attention, talent, and organizational patience in things that can compound.

The real question is not whether AI can produce a dazzling demo, but whether it can improve the probability, cost, speed, and quality of therapeutic discovery. Can it reduce meaningless exploration? Can it help identify better patient populations? Can it connect mechanism, molecule, and clinical need more intelligently? Can it make pharmaceutical R&D less wasteful and more humane?

The answer will not be immediate. Pharmaceutical innovation has long cycles. A true productivity revolution in drug discovery will be slower than a consumer-app launch and harder to measure than a benchmark score. But that is precisely why long-term thinking matters.

## Conclusion

Every bubble contains illusion. Some illusions vanish. Others leave behind roads, rails, cables, data centers, and new forms of production. The task for AI drug discovery is to avoid becoming merely a beneficiary of speculative narrative and instead become part of the deployment period of AI.

To be a long-term value investor in AI drug discovery is to believe neither blindly in the bubble nor cynically against it. It is to ask which capabilities will remain after the heat fades, and to build for that future.
