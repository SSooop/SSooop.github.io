On July 3, 2025, CB Insights published "Pharma AI readiness: How the 50 largest companies by market cap stack up," presenting an AI Readiness Index for major pharmaceutical companies.

Lilly, the world's largest pharmaceutical company by market capitalization as of September 28, 2025, ranked first in that list. Interestingly, CB Insights updated the ranking on August 8 and moved Roche to the top, which made the ranking change itself worth watching. From the perspective of industry observation and the strategic moves of multinational pharmaceutical companies, many practitioners share one impression: Sanofi is all in on AI. If there is a chance, I would like to compare Sanofi and Lilly more deeply in a future article.

For large companies with strong cash flow, the central challenge in strategic transformation often comes less from external market pressure and more from internal organizational inertia. Cash-rich companies can use equity investment, structured strategic collaboration, upfront payments, milestones, platform partnerships, and acquisitions to fill capability gaps quickly.

Yet unlike traditional collaboration models that multinational pharma companies know well, the true value of AI strategy often requires deep organizational change. Decision processes, talent structures, and cross-functional collaboration all need to be rebuilt. This difficulty gives AI-native biotech companies room to survive and grow under the shadow of giants. Even so, Lilly's AI strategy shows impressive foresight and execution depth.

Since 2022, Lilly's AI transformation and external collaboration strategy has shown three main characteristics:

1. It remains focused on core therapeutic areas and keeps product value first.
2. It treats AI as a supplement to internal capabilities rather than only as a broad platform slogan.
3. It encourages atomic entrepreneurship within functional departments while using the company brand to build an ecosystem moat.

Lilly's AI strategy is not a simple buying spree. Its collaborations tend to connect with real therapeutic and discovery needs. Partnerships with companies such as Nimbus and Isomorphic Labs show a willingness to absorb frontier AI capability while keeping the strategic center close to product development.

This is different from a purely exploratory corporate venture portfolio. Lilly appears to ask whether a technology can strengthen a concrete discovery or development path. This product-first logic may be one reason its AI strategy looks more grounded than many platform narratives.

For a pharmaceutical giant, the most valuable AI collaboration is not necessarily the most fashionable model. It is the one that can integrate with internal disease-area strategy, data assets, experimental capability, and clinical-development priorities.

Lilly's Catalyze360 strategy reflects another layer of thinking. Instead of treating AI only as an internal productivity tool, Lilly is trying to build a broader innovation ecosystem. A large pharmaceutical company has brand, capital, manufacturing, clinical, and regulatory credibility. If it can connect those resources with emerging AI companies, it can become an organizing node in the ecosystem.

This is particularly important because AI-native companies often have strong technical capability but lack the full development infrastructure required for drugs. Lilly can provide an industrial pathway. In return, the company gains early access to novel tools, molecules, and teams.

Lilly's AI strategy also needs to be understood in relation to its therapeutic and modality strengths. In metabolic disease, immunology, oncology, and antibody-related technologies, AI may help with candidate generation, developability assessment, biomarker strategy, and clinical-data interpretation.

For antibodies and new modalities, AI is not just about structure prediction. It can influence sequence design, affinity tuning, epitope strategy, manufacturability, and safety-risk assessment. The key is whether these tools are embedded into real decision loops.

Sanofi's AI posture often feels more explicitly ecosystem-driven and transformation-oriented. Lilly's posture, at least from the outside, appears more product-centered and therapeutically focused. Roche, meanwhile, has deep data and diagnostics assets that give it a different kind of AI foundation.

These companies may all be "doing AI," but the strategic meaning differs. Some use AI to improve existing R&D. Some use AI to reshape data infrastructure. Some use AI to build external ecosystems. The winner will not be the company with the loudest AI narrative, but the company that converts AI into better decisions repeatedly.

Lilly's AI ambition is not simply defensive. It is an offensive attempt to compete for the high ground of pharmaceutical R&D in the AI era. The company is not abandoning its traditional strengths. It is trying to extend them through AI.

The real question is whether a large organization can turn AI capability into organizational behavior. If it can, giants like Lilly may catch up quickly with many AI-native startups in model capability and industrial deployment. If it cannot, its AI strategy may remain a collection of impressive partnerships.

For the next few years, Lilly will be one of the most important cases to watch in pharma AI.
