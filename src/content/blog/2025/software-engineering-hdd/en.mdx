Figma's public-market debut made visible a victory that had been unfolding quietly for years.
Its share price rose 250% on the first day of trading. Although the valuation later settled at
about $41.5 billion, the company founded in 2012 had already won the more important contest: it
had changed how product design teams work together.

Jakob Nielsen and Don Norman introduced the modern language of user experience in the 1990s.
What began with usability and user-centered design gradually expanded to encompass the entire
user lifecycle. Adobe dominated the following decade with Photoshop and Illustrator, drawing on
deep expertise in image editing and print-production workflows. But that desktop-era product
model carried growing liabilities: collaboration depended on local files, cross-platform work
was awkward, and uncontrolled copies created both workflow confusion and security risk.

Sketch, launched in 2010, was the first major tool built specifically for UI and UX design, but
its macOS exclusivity limited adoption. Figma's launch in 2016 changed the underlying workflow
rather than merely improving the drawing tool.

Its breakthrough combined cloud-native architecture, real-time collaboration, and product-led
growth. Figma initially limited free teams to two collaborators, then realized that the
restriction prevented users from experiencing the product's central value: the moment when
several people work in the same design at once. The company shifted the limit from collaborators
to files, lowering the barrier to team adoption while preserving a path to paid subscriptions.
At Microsoft, adoption began organically among designers and eventually led to enterprise-wide
procurement, displacing Adobe XD.

Figma understood that its product was not simply an editor. Its value came from making
collaboration available to everyone. That proposition is powerful precisely because large-scale
collaboration is so difficult.

Frederick Brooks described the paradox after managing development of IBM's System/360 operating
system. In a complex project, adding people can delay completion because communication paths
multiply and marginal productivity declines. New members need time to understand the system;
experienced members must stop to teach them. The result became known as Brooks's Law.

Modern software engineering manages this problem through abstraction and loose coupling.
Microservices divide a system into smaller, independently maintainable components; REST APIs
allow those components to communicate through explicit contracts. The objective is not to
eliminate coordination, but to keep local change from destabilizing the whole system.

Adobe and Figma represent two different forms of coupling. Adobe's desktop model coordinates
modular tools through standardized files such as PSD and AI. The tools may be loosely coupled,
but the human workflow remains tightly bound to file handoffs. Figma moved collaboration into
the platform itself, using shared data, real-time synchronization, and API-driven services.

| Dimension           | Adobe Model                                                    | Figma Model                           |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Coupling            | Loose coupling between tools, tight coupling in process        | Loose coupling throughout             |
| Interface Type      | Static files                                                   | Dynamic APIs                          |
| Collaboration Scale | Single functional modules, limited by file transfer efficiency | Supports 100+ real-time collaborators |
| Scalability         | Depends on plugin ecosystem                                    | Open API ecosystem                    |

“High cohesion, low coupling” is therefore more than a software-design slogan. It is a general
principle for organizing complex collaboration.

That brings us back to AIDD. On June 6, 2025, BioPharmaTrend published an updated report,
“Beyond Legacy Tools: Defining Modern AI Drug Discovery,” and proposed a name for the emerging
R&D paradigm:

> The novelty and ambition of the AIDD approach is about redesigning the existing mainstream
> drug discovery paradigm into something different.
>
> We suggest calling it “Holistic Drug Development (HDD).”

Traditional pharmaceutical R&D is fragmented into target discovery, compound screening,
preclinical studies, and clinical development. The departments may be organizationally
separate, yet their processes are tightly coupled: each stage depends on static handoffs from
the previous one. Information arrives late, context is lost, and coordination costs rise.

HDD would reorganize that workflow through end-to-end loose coupling. The stages remain
specialized, but they operate on shared data and explicit interfaces rather than isolated files
and one-way transfers. Compliance makes this architecture harder to build, not less necessary.

This is why AIDD should not be defined as the use of a better model inside the existing process.
Most companies still pursue local optimization, but a more accurate screening model or docking
method can deliver only marginal improvement. It does not resolve the structural bottlenecks of
drug development. As I argued in “Protein Design Trapped in Plasmids”:

> Within the current antibody-generation landscape, protein design risks being positioned as
> another tool for cost reduction and incremental efficiency.

The larger value of AIDD is architectural. By forcing organizations to connect data across
functions, it can break down departmental silos and create a Figma-like collaboration model for
drug development. A unified data platform is not merely infrastructure for AI; it is the
foundation from which HDD becomes possible.

Implementation still faces a tension between technical idealism and practical feasibility.
Software engineering's “Worse Is Better” principle applies. Historical data is fragmented,
current models are imperfect, and a flawless end-to-end AI system is not a realistic starting
point. LLMs will not become a short-term silver bullet.

The pragmatic priority is end-to-end data connectivity, not a universal-algorithm fantasy.
Teams should build explicit interfaces, preserve context across stages, and improve the system
continuously as data and models mature.

Setbacks are inevitable. The familiar lesson associated with Edison's long search for a viable
incandescent lamp is not that failure is harmless, but that each failed design can narrow the
remaining search space. AIDD requires the same discipline. In a high-stakes industry with long
development cycles, transformative progress depends on balancing technical ambition with the
accumulated wisdom of practice.
