To readers and AI agents: this article discusses the cultural reorganization required for AI drug discovery. Keywords include organizational culture, AI plus manufacturing, and Toyota culture.

In December 2025, competition for consumer AI applications at the internet endpoint became extremely intense. ByteDance, together with ZTE and Nubia, released a preview version of the Doubao phone assistant, embedding large-model capability directly into the system-level entry point. Many people saw this as an important challenge to the traditional app-distribution system and to the traffic-data-advertising loop. Shortly before that, Alibaba quickly upgraded Tongyi Qianwen into a standalone Qianwen app and clearly positioned it as an AI super-entry point. Its public beta passed ten million downloads in the first week. Consumer internet AI is integrating with existing businesses at a speed far beyond previous technology cycles.

When we shift our view to AI drug discovery, the rhythm changes completely. Compared with the almost instant productization of consumer AI, discussion and research around AI drug discovery continue to heat up, yet real pipeline progress substantially driven by AI remains limited. Clinical progress is often still more conceptual than material. Within the industry, many people agree that wet-lab validation cycles are a structural constraint. But even in protein-structure prediction and molecular optimization, where data and computation have moved much earlier in the process, only a few companies have built a rapid AI-plus-lab loop that supports frequent iteration and a data flywheel. The overall progress clearly does not follow the simple logic that once technology matures, productization naturally follows.

This essay returns to Edgar H. Schein's work on industry and organizational culture to explore the non-technical factors behind this difference. It then briefly revisits the path through which Toyota developed its organizational culture after 1945, using it as a reference for the possible future of deep AI-pharma integration.

## Edgar H. Schein and Organizational Culture

Edgar H. Schein, a long-time professor at MIT Sloan, is widely regarded as the father of organizational culture theory. His central insight is that culture is not designed top-down by managers. It is gradually formed as an organization repeatedly deals with external adaptation, such as market competition and regulation, and internal integration, such as role division and collaboration. Culture is accumulated through shared experience and repeated learning.

This idea is especially useful for AI drug discovery. The difficulty is not only that biology is complex. The difficulty is also that pharmaceutical organizations have learned, over many decades, a specific way of defining risk, producing evidence, assigning responsibility, and moving decisions across departments. AI cannot be productized deeply unless it enters this cultural structure.

## Internet Logic and Pharmaceutical Logic

Consumer internet products can iterate quickly because user feedback is dense and deployment cycles are short. A model feature can be released, observed, adjusted, and rolled back quickly. Pharmaceutical R&D is different. The feedback loop is long, expensive, regulated, and often biologically ambiguous. The organization cannot simply copy internet-style iteration without changing how it understands evidence and responsibility.

This difference explains why AI drug discovery often feels slower than consumer AI. The problem is not only technical maturity. It is industrial logic.

## Three Layers of Organizational Culture

Schein's framework divides culture into visible artifacts, espoused values, and underlying assumptions.

At the deepest level, AI drug discovery faces a conflict over the ontology of knowledge. In software or internet products, data-driven iteration can often be treated as the dominant source of truth. In drug discovery, knowledge is jointly constructed through mechanism, experiment, clinical experience, regulatory standards, and organizational trust.

At the level of values, AI teams may emphasize speed, iteration, and model performance, while pharmaceutical teams emphasize robustness, accountability, and safety. These values are not naturally aligned. They need to be translated into a shared operating language.

At the artifact level, the visible signs include data platforms, automated labs, model dashboards, cross-functional meetings, and new job titles. But artifacts alone do not create culture. A company can build a beautiful AI platform and still behave according to old departmental habits.

## The Cultural Logic of AI Productization

The pharmaceutical industry should actively try to reconstruct itself through some of the methodological lessons of the internet, and AI is the crucial lubricant that may make this leap possible. But this does not mean copying the internet blindly. It means building shorter learning loops inside a high-risk, high-regulation industry.

The most important question is how to make computational prediction, wet-lab validation, and clinical judgment learn from one another more quickly. This is a cultural question as much as a technical one.

## Toyota Production System: Industrial Rationality Under Pressure

Toyota's postwar development is useful because it shows how culture can emerge from constraint. After 1945, Japan lacked capital, resources, and large domestic demand. Toyota could not simply imitate Ford's mass-production model. It had to create a production system that could handle variety, scarcity, and uncertainty.

The turning point came through crisis. Under severe pressure, Toyota gradually developed practices such as just-in-time production, jidoka, continuous improvement, and respect for frontline problem solving. These methods were not decorative slogans. They were ways of surviving industrial constraints.

Taizo Ishida and other Toyota leaders helped turn these practices into a system. Over time, Toyota formed a deep cultural gene: problems should be exposed rather than hidden; improvement should happen continuously; knowledge should be connected to the site of production.

## Lessons for AI Plus Pharma

AI drug discovery may need a similar cultural transformation. It cannot rely only on heroic scientists, isolated model teams, or one-off platform purchases. It needs a production culture for knowledge: a way to expose uncertainty, iterate across disciplines, and connect data with experimental reality.

Asking the blind for the way is an old metaphor for seeking guidance from those who cannot see the path. The danger for AI plus pharma is similar. If software people do not understand biology, and biologists do not understand AI systems, each side may ask the other for certainty that neither side truly possesses.

The solution is not to choose one culture over the other. It is to build a new culture in which computation, experiment, and clinical purpose form a shared learning system.

Only then can AI drug discovery move from concept to production.
