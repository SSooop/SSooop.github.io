On August 7, 2025, GPT-5 was released with enormous public attention. For a moment, the debate around AGI entered a strange mixture of heat and cold. OpenAI described the model as having PhD-level reasoning, while many users complained that benchmark performance did not equal actual experience. The launch-day slide incident, where a simple numerical comparison became a meme, made the reaction even more complicated.

I therefore paid attention to Polymarket to see whether the market, in a statistical sense, believed the "PhD-level reasoning" story. The result was striking: the odds between Google and OpenAI reversed dramatically on the day of release. By August 31, Google had reached an astonishing 99.4 percent chance in that market.

The core reason is that public expectations around AGI changed significantly before and after the GPT-5 release. Once people realized that the supposedly overwhelming intelligence might also be a form of marketing, traditional internet factors such as market share, user habit, product distribution, and ecosystem advantage returned to the center of attention. Google, with its massive user base and application ecosystem, clearly holds a major advantage at the application layer. Its underestimated potential may be fully released in the next stage of AI competition.

From a commercialization perspective, 2025 has often been called the first year of AI-agent commercialization. The field is developing along multiple paths. Single agents pursue broad task execution. Multi-agent collaboration systems use division of labor and specialization to handle complex scenarios. Lightweight task-management agents emphasize efficiency and low resource consumption in narrow use cases. Looking at the first half of 2025, several application trends stand out:

- **Voice agents** seek to transform human-computer interaction beyond text.
- **Agent RAG** focuses on enterprise services and new modes of internal query.
- **AI agent protocols** try to standardize agent interaction and enable multi-agent ecosystems.
- **Computer-using agents** point toward AI-native operating systems.
- **Coding agents** automate software generation and reshape engineering workflows.
- **Deep research agents** automate information mining and strategic analysis for enterprise customers.

Among these, coding agents may move fastest because they have the clearest reward structure. Code can run, tests can fail, and errors are visible. That makes software engineering a valuable reference field for every vertical AI practitioner. Deep research agents, meanwhile, may bring unprecedented pressure to enterprise information providers and consulting firms.

## Why Research Needs an AI-Native System

The existing research paradigm is still largely human centered. We ask human researchers to define the question, search the literature, form hypotheses, design experiments, analyze results, and synthesize conclusions. AI is often added as a tool inside this old structure. But if AI has different strengths from humans, the research system should not simply preserve the old workflow.

An AI-native research system should match AI's strengths rather than only compensate for human weakness. It should allow models to explore complex reasoning spaces more broadly, compare many possible explanations, maintain long-range memory, and repeatedly refine hypotheses. The goal is not to replace scientists, but to let scientists indirectly grasp cognitive territories beyond their own working memory and search capacity.

This is especially important in AI for Science. Scientific research often involves open-ended questions, uncertain pathways, and path-dependent exploration. A rigid workflow cannot capture this process. But a purely autonomous agent may also drift. The challenge is to build a system that combines exploration with verification.

## Lessons from Coding Agents

Coding agents are useful because they show what happens when AI is connected to a tight feedback loop. A model writes code. The system runs tests. The output either works or fails. This gives the agent a way to iterate rapidly. The environment supplies a reward signal that is clearer than most research settings.

Scientific research rarely has such direct feedback. A biological hypothesis may take months to test. A data interpretation may depend on assumptions that are not immediately visible. A wet-lab result may be confounded by experimental noise. Therefore, an AI-native research system must deliberately construct intermediate feedback loops: literature consistency checks, data provenance checks, simulation, retrospective validation, and structured critique.

## The Role of Deep Research Agents

Deep research agents can help by absorbing large amounts of information and turning fragmented material into structured hypotheses. In enterprise settings, this may threaten traditional consulting and research-report workflows. But in scientific work, the value is not merely summarization. The value lies in building a persistent research memory that can connect scattered facts across time.

A good research agent should know what has already been tried, why it failed, what assumptions remain hidden, and which question should be asked next. That is different from producing a polished report. It is closer to building a thinking environment.

## Designing for Human Judgment

The most important principle is that AI-native research should not remove human judgment. It should move human judgment to a higher level. Instead of spending most of their time searching, formatting, and recombining information, researchers should spend more time defining the problem, evaluating evidence, and deciding which uncertainty is worth resolving.

In this sense, the AI scientist is not a single model. It is a system composed of models, memory, tools, data, human review, and institutional learning. The real product is not an answer, but a research loop that improves over time.

## Conclusion

The future of AI in research will not be determined only by model intelligence. It will be determined by system design. Software engineering is moving quickly because it already has executable artifacts and dense feedback. Scientific research must build its own equivalent loops.

An AI-native research system should help humans explore more hypotheses, remember more context, verify more rigorously, and define better questions. Only then can AI move from being an impressive assistant to becoming part of the structure of discovery itself.
