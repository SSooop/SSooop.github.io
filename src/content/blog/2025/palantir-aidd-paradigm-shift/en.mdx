The first half of 2025 brought strong growth in AI-agent applications, with AI coding producing
some of the clearest commercial breakthroughs. Publicly available figures put Cursor's annual
recurring revenue at $500 million—a result that stands apart from much of the broader agent
market.

The reasons are structural. Agents outside programming still struggle to adapt to messy
real-world environments. Coding, by contrast, offers standardized tasks, measurable returns on
investment, and customers with a clear willingness to pay for productivity. It is also a
relatively asset-light business model, similar to models already validated across consumer
internet markets. If the current trajectory continues, AI commercialization should spread
quickly through e-commerce, content production, advertising, and education.

Capital-intensive industries are a different test. Their data, operations, and decision rights
are embedded in physical assets, regulated processes, and long delivery cycles. Success in
software does not automatically transfer. Palantir's performance offers a useful reference for
how AI might cross that boundary.

Palantir's advantage is generally attributed to two capabilities: a sophisticated Ontology
architecture and an unusual capacity for vertical integration. Together, they allow the company
to address complex operating problems in government, financial services, and industrial
manufacturing—domains in which it often faces few direct competitors.

## How Ontology Deconstructs Industry Complexity

Palantir's main product suite includes Gotham, Foundry, Apollo, and AIP. AIP sits on top of the
company's mature data infrastructure. If we set aside Apollo's operational automation and the
security advantages Palantir developed for government customers, Foundry is best understood as
an enterprise data-operations platform.

Its defining feature is the use of an ontology to model business entities and improve decision
systems. Instead of treating enterprise data as disconnected tables, the Ontology represents
assets, transactions, people, and processes as objects with explicit relationships, logic, and
permitted actions.

The architecture can be understood through three layers:

| Layer    | Role                                         | Implementation                                                                   |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Semantic | Establish a shared enterprise data language  | Define object types such as assets and transactions, their properties, and links |
| Kinetic  | Turn business rules into responsive logic    | Embed configurable rules and industry-specific actions in the Ontology           |
| Dynamic  | Support optimization and augmented decisions | Analyze live data with machine learning, simulation, and scenario reasoning      |

The **Semantic Layer** creates a common language for the enterprise. It standardizes how teams
describe objects and their relationships, so an “asset,” “transaction,” or “operational event”
means the same thing across functions.

The **Kinetic Layer** makes that model operational. Rules engines and modular business logic
connect the Ontology to real-time actions, allowing an organization to change operating rules
without rebuilding the entire system.

The **Dynamic Layer** adds continuous optimization and decision support. Machine-learning models
analyze live data, while simulation and scenario tools turn those signals into actionable
choices.

The problem is that many enterprise efficiency gains attributed to AI actually begin with
unfinished digital transformation. In a local use case, it can be difficult to separate the
contribution of AI from the basic benefit of finally organizing the data and workflow.

Consider a skincare experiment in which someone introduces a new product while also beginning
to sleep regularly and cleanse properly. If their skin improves, isolating the product's effect
from the combined effect of sleep and cleansing is nearly impossible. Enterprise AI often has
the same attribution problem.

This is why Palantir's systems-integration capability matters. Even without treating AI models
as a technical moat, the company has a differentiated ability to help organizations complete the
underlying digital transformation on which useful AI depends.

## Commercial Moats: Leadership Through Project Delivery

Palantir's early backing from In-Q-Tel, the CIA's venture-capital arm, helped establish the
company as a core supplier for counterterrorism and intelligence analysis. Its systems have been
associated with critical missions, including the effort to locate Osama bin Laden. Government
contracts accounted for 55% of revenue in 2024, and long relationships with clients such as the
Department of Defense and the FBI created an exceptional trust barrier.

That history is difficult to reproduce outside government. Two other elements of Palantir's
model are more transferable:

1. **Forward Deployed Software Engineers (FDSEs):** Palantir embeds engineers with customers.
   They participate directly in data integration and decision workflows, combining software with
   high-touch consulting.
2. **Ontology SDK-driven modularity:** the Ontology turns data, logic, and actions into reusable
   modules—object types, links, rules, and operations—that make new applications faster to build.
   This flexibility has supported use cases across more than 90 industries, from fraud detection
   to healthcare analytics. Palantir's commercial customer count grew 29% year over year in 2024.

At a time when AI leaders face persistent executive questions about ROI, end-to-end project
delivery offers a compelling answer: do not sell capability alone; deliver a measurable
operating result.

For AIDD, however, this answer creates a deeper strategic question. If an AI SaaS provider can
take responsibility for a complete R&D project, the pharmaceutical value chain begins to shift.
When an external provider can perform work close to the core of drug discovery, a pharma company
must reconsider where its own competitive advantage actually resides.

The same issue has surfaced in my conversations with leading laboratory-automation suppliers in
China. Some have assembled early drug-discovery teams not because they intend to become pharma
companies, but because real projects are the most convincing way to demonstrate how automation
improves R&D efficiency—and therefore to sell the equipment.

Can an AIDD company simply copy Palantir? Earlier versions of this proposition appeared during
the internet-healthcare startup cycle around 2000. Once AIP is removed, much of Palantir's model
is the scaled deployment of digital systems in industrial settings. Pharma has pursued that
transformation for decades. Multinational companies have used laboratory automation to build
advantages in early discovery for more than 20 years, but those advantages diminish across
biomedicine's long, multistage development cycle.

The deeper problem is synchronization. Molecular biology and digital technology evolved at very
different speeds between 2000 and 2025. Bringing two rapidly changing domains together creates
commercial implementation problems for which neither organizational design nor talent systems
yet offer a mature template.

The experience of AI coding provides one final clue. After two years building Devin, the
Cognition team advised developers not to over-optimize agent middleware. Their practical
guidance favors integrated architectures and reasonable fault-tolerance thresholds so that
engineering teams can preserve speed while the underlying technology continues to change.

That is a clear case of “Worse Is Better.” AIDD may need the same discipline: enough integration
to deliver an outcome, enough tolerance to survive imperfect data and models, and enough domain
expertise to know where simplification is safe.

Investors concentrate on selection; industry practitioners concentrate on persistence. The
future of AIDD will be shaped by people capable of making a sound choice—and then staying with
the problem long enough to make it work.
