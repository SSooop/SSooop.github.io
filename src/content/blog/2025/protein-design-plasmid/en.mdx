In late June, Chai Discovery released Chai-2 and its supporting research on bioRxiv. The model
marked an important advance in antibody design: it produced experimentally testable candidates
at the scale of a 24-well plate. Since AlphaFold, protein engineering has been moving from
predicting structures toward designing functions. Chai-2 makes that transition unusually
concrete.

The purpose of this article is to trace the technical logic behind that shift and ask what it
means for non-price competition in AI-driven drug discovery.

The review “Computational Protein Design” provides a useful starting point. It distinguishes
how generative methods operate across two very different spaces: discrete protein sequences and
continuous three-dimensional structures.

Inverse-folding methods tend to perform better when the desired protein function is precisely
defined. Protein datasets are far more biased and sparse than natural-language corpora, and
protein design cannot be evaluated computationally alone. Every candidate must eventually pass
through expression and functional assays. The model is therefore being asked to learn a mapping
across sequence, structure, and experiment—not merely a statistical pattern within one modality.

The central difficulty is that we cannot enumerate every three-dimensional structure capable of
producing a desired function. If sequence determines structure and structure constrains
function, then the probability of a sequence given a function must be marginalized over a vast
space of possible backbones:

Most practical pipelines first estimate `P(bb | function)`, then perform inverse folding
inside a restricted backbone space. This is why workflows such as RFdiffusion followed by
ProteinMPNN often cluster generated scaffolds before sequence design: diversity must be managed
before the search becomes computationally or experimentally intractable.

Minibinder design illustrates the constraint. The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of a
target correlates strongly with achievable binding affinity. AlphaProteo's emphasis on success
against convex surfaces is therefore not a presentation detail; it reflects the geometry of the
problem.

Antibodies are harder. Ig-like scaffolds are attractive because their developability and
druggability have already been validated clinically, but antibody function is concentrated in
the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs). Unlike the relatively regular alpha helices and
beta sheets common in minibinders, CDR loops are conformationally flexible. That flexibility
makes `P(bb | function)` much more difficult to estimate.

The experimental search space compounds the problem. A de novo antibody design must coordinate
six CDRs, often treated as four design blocks: HCDR1, HCDR2/3, LCDR1, and LCDR2/3. A yeast-display
library with a nominal throughput of 10⁸ sounds enormous. But if that capacity must cover
four independently varying blocks, the effective depth per block is only the fourth root of
10⁸—about 100 possibilities.

That exponential collapse is the plasmid bottleneck. Even with chip-synthesized oligonucleotide
libraries and trinucleotide synthesis, correctly assembled, full-length plasmid libraries remain
difficult to produce at the required diversity and fidelity. It also explains why early
antibody-design studies tended to optimize HCDR3 alone: every additional jointly designed region
multiplies the experimental burden.

Chai-2 changes the calculation by showing that, for structurally well-characterized targets, a
model can produce lead sequences directly without first screening a large library. Its work on
interfaces in trimeric proteins such as TNF-alpha is especially revealing. The binding surface
formed between subunits is flat and highly polar, making it difficult to address with simple
geometric rules. Yet such interfaces can carry distinctive therapeutic value—the same broad
strategy is visible in Roche's development of RVT-3101.

Computationally designed antibodies still face developability challenges, particularly in CDRs
that have not passed through natural selection in vivo. But Chai-2 demonstrates a meaningful
advantage in the estimation of `P(bb | function)`. For targets with well-defined structures
and epitopes, the next-generation workflow may take three stages:

1. use generative antibody design to create high-quality starting points;
2. apply inverse folding to construct focused optimization libraries; and
3. use reinforcement screening to impose experimental selection pressure.

This is not the end of screening. It is a change in where screening begins.

Antibody-lead discovery CROs have already made conventional discovery cheaper. As a result,
antibody design risks being positioned as another tool for cost reduction and incremental
efficiency. The more important question is whether protein design can escape that narrow value
proposition.

The history of the mobile phone offers a useful analogy. A telephone became a new computing
platform only when communications, internet connectivity, and processing converged. Protein
design will require a similar convergence. Generative models can enrich the search space with
plausible structures; high-throughput screening can apply reality's selection pressure. Each
approach compensates for the other's weakness.

Together, they may enable molecular designs that conventional biology would struggle to reach:
asymmetric proteins, context-dependent binders, or proteins with dynamic responses to their
environment. That is more than a faster antibody-discovery workflow. It is a path toward
functions that existing molecular repertoires do not provide.
