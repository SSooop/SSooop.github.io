Not long ago, while chatting with a friend in investment banking, we talked about the difference between the words **drug** and **medicine**. It is a surprisingly interesting distinction. In academia and industry, drug has long been the conventional word for a compound under development. Yet outside medical contexts, drug can also refer to illegal or addictive substances. That ambiguity creates a public perception problem, and sometimes makes pharmaceutical companies look like institutions serving profit alone.

Changing a word does not solve the problem. The history of the industry and the structure of modern society have produced the reality we see today. Pharmaceutical companies have gradually become more profit-oriented and less visibly centered on the pursuit of pure therapeutic benefit for several reasons:

1. **Pressure on R&D returns**: New drug development is long, expensive, and uncertain. A typical program may take 10-15 years, cost more than one billion dollars, and still carry a failure rate above 90%. Patent protection is finite, usually around 20 years, so companies must recover costs within a limited commercial window. This pushes them toward short-term profitability and away from a stubborn pursuit of the "perfect therapy."

2. **Capital-driven incentives**: Investors prefer high-margin assets, which pushes resources toward blockbuster drugs. The reason a blockbuster is "blockbuster" is usually broad indication coverage. At its core, this is still a question of supply and demand.

The explosive growth of the autoimmune star product Dupi came largely from continuous indication expansion. After its initial approval for atopic dermatitis, it expanded into asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, eosinophilic esophagitis, and other indications. This both extends commercial life and expands demand. From a market-value perspective, the logic is hard to deny.

Looking back at today's broad-indication drug development trend, it is both a commercial choice and a natural consequence of scientific progress. It is not necessarily wrong. Rare diseases are not completely without treatment either. In some sense, the FDA and industrial capital are also making a practical ethical compromise around how to solve human health problems.

AIDD offers a handle for addressing these issues. But the industry, represented by the wave after AlphaFold, seems to have fallen into a kind of self-justifying loop: focusing on the recognition of structurally special targets in structural biology, while insufficiently examining the clinical necessity of target selection. The result can feel like a technically elegant answer to a question that was not carefully asked.

As an example, let us look at the target competition around B cell depletion therapies, which may reshape autoimmune treatment. The figure comes from _The race to reset autoimmune diseases_.

Deep B cell depletion can cause serious adverse effects. After all, it essentially knocks down a major part of the immune system. The future direction may be to focus on depletion of specific B cell subtypes. From a purely technical perspective, **AI can play a decisive role here, not by merely predicting a particular structure, but by helping define better therapeutic choices.**

Returning to the business side, the strange result is that both listed AIDD leaders and Silicon Valley startups often over-focus on model-building itself. They repeatedly emphasize AI model performance, but do not always return to a clinically demand-centered path of technological innovation. Investor expectations naturally reflect this imbalance.

The steam engine was first used for pumping water out of coal mines. After Watt improved it, it was applied to rail transport and reshaped humanity's sense of time and space. Transportation costs fell dramatically, global trade networks emerged, demand grew across steel and textiles, and even the standardization of time zones in 1884 was part of the same chain reaction. This is a vivid example of the economic principle that demand creates supply, and supply also creates demand.

Returning to AIDD, before shouting that "AI will disrupt the industry," we may need to answer a more basic question: what should the reshaped industry actually look like?

I still remember an episode of Silicon Valley 101 from a couple of months ago joking that many current AI Agent innovations look like horse-drawn cars rather than cars with wheels. Recently, I also saw Gartner's 2025 AI hype cycle, which is worth sharing here.

It is clear that answering what the reshaped industry should look like is not easy. The long march has only begun, and we are still at a very early stage. But if meeting unmet human needs is an enduring principle, then answering **"what exactly is clinically unmet need"** through digital and scientific means should be the hard truth of AIDD.

How can a patient's quality of life be improved?
How should we distinguish disease courses more precisely?
How can we concretely improve both the length and quality of life for patients with cancer?

These questions require scientists from many different disciplines to keep moving forward. Only then can the dream become reality. In the pharmaceutical industry, AI will ultimately act more like the catalyst of a chain reaction, giving scientists more time and better tools to think through the questions that matter.

In the end, I still hope AIDD can continue to drive medical innovation, improve human life, and help rewrite **drug** into **medicine**.
