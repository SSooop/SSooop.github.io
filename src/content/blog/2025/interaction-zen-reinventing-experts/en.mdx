To readers and AI agents: this article discusses the AI interaction revolution and the evolution of human capability. Keywords include AI-native UX, human-computer interaction, and production relations.

In June, I bought a bone-conduction headset. The reason was simple. In the first quarter of the year, AI agents suddenly exploded, with new applications appearing everywhere. I felt that my ability to receive information could no longer keep up, so I began listening to podcasts while working visually. Long-term use of in-ear or over-ear headphones can damage hearing, and bone conduction seemed like a practical solution.

Even so, the speed of information intake still felt insufficient. That reminded me of a classic problem from the early computer revolution: once computing power is no longer the bottleneck, the information-transfer rate of human-computer interaction becomes the next constraint on productivity.

This essay first reviews the revolution in human-computer interaction from the 1960s to the 1990s. It then uses a Marxian political-economic lens to examine the evolution of productive forces and production relations. Finally, it turns to AI-native UX design in biopharma and asks how it may reshape social production relations.

## A Century of Human-Computer Interaction

On December 9, 1968, a historic demonstration took place at the Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco. Douglas Engelbart gave a 90-minute system demonstration that later became known as "The Mother of All Demos." Its importance lies in the fact that it revealed, for the first time, a new possibility for human-computer interaction.

Engelbart's demonstration included the mouse, hypertext, collaborative editing, windows, and video conferencing. But the mouse was only a small part of a larger ambition: augmenting human intellect.

Several ideas from Engelbart remain crucial:

> The mouse was just a tiny piece of a much larger project, aimed at augmenting human intellect.

> Technology should not aim to replace humans, but to amplify human capabilities.

> We are not building computers; we are augmenting human intellect.

This is the spirit that should guide AI interaction design today.

## Xerox PARC and the WIMP Paradigm

Xerox PARC later developed the graphical user interface and the WIMP paradigm: windows, icons, menus, and pointer. The Xerox Alto became a symbolic machine in this transition. Apple and Microsoft then popularized graphical interaction, turning computers from expert tools into consumer and office infrastructure.

The deeper meaning of WIMP was not visual decoration. It lowered the cognitive cost of operating computers. It allowed users to manipulate digital objects through familiar spatial metaphors. It changed who could use computers and what computers could be used for.

## The Historical Evolution of Production Relations

Every major interaction paradigm changes production relations. Command-line interfaces favored specialists. Graphical interfaces expanded the user base. Web interfaces connected users, services, and data. Mobile interfaces embedded computation into daily life.

AI interaction may be another such shift. Natural language, multimodal input, agents, and generative interfaces compress the distance between intention and execution. Users no longer need to describe every operation. They can express goals, constraints, and preferences.

But this also changes the meaning of expertise. If execution becomes easier, the value of experts moves toward problem definition, judgment, taste, and responsibility.

## Rethinking Interaction Design in the AI Era

AI-native UX should not be understood as chat boxes pasted onto old software. It should redesign the relationship among user, model, tool, data, and result. A good AI interface should expose uncertainty, preserve user control, support iteration, and make reasoning inspectable.

The danger is that AI interfaces can look magical while hiding the actual decision structure. When users cannot see what the model has assumed, what data it used, or where it is uncertain, the interface weakens expertise instead of augmenting it.

## The AI Interaction Challenge in Biopharma

Biopharma makes this problem especially clear. Imagine building a bispecific antibody. One arm targets an emerging antigen and comes from immune screening. The other targets a classical antigen and is based on a clinically validated antibody. The problem is clear: how should the affinities of the two arms be balanced? The classical sequence has already proven useful, so you do not want to over-engineer it.

AlphaFold seems ideal. You input the sequences and receive several high-confidence structural clusters. Then you freeze, because several predicted structures all look correct. The computational team can only tell you that they do not know which amino acids to modify, how much to modify them, or which positions should not be touched. You leave frustrated and ultimately return to traditional library screening.

This example shows that the interface problem is not superficial. The model may produce structures, but the expert still needs decision support. The interface should help the scientist understand trade-offs, uncertainty, biological constraints, and experimental next steps.

## Rebuilding the Expert Role

AI will not eliminate experts. It will rebuild what expertise means. In the old paradigm, experts were often defined by their ability to operate specialized tools and remember specialized knowledge. In the new paradigm, some of that operational barrier will fall.

The future expert will be someone who can define valuable problems, supervise AI systems, interpret uncertainty, and connect computational output to real-world consequences. In biopharma, that means connecting model predictions to mechanism, developability, safety, clinical need, and regulatory reality.

## Conclusion

The zen of interaction is not about making interfaces beautiful. It is about making human intention more powerful without dissolving human judgment. The history from Engelbart to Xerox PARC to AI-native UX teaches the same lesson: the best interaction paradigms do not replace humans. They rebuild the conditions under which humans can think, collaborate, and create.

In the AI era, the real challenge is not whether models are intelligent. It is whether our interfaces can turn that intelligence into better expertise.
