On September 8, DeepMind released a preprint on arXiv titled "An AI system to help scientists write expert-level empirical software." It is impressive that, amid the rapid development of AI agents, DeepMind continues to focus on foundational questions in AI for Science. In an earlier article on AI-native research systems, I wrote:

> The existing research paradigm needs to be overturned. It should match AI's strengths rather than human strengths, and should try to explore and exhaust complex reasoning patterns so that human scientists can indirectly grasp cognitive territories beyond their own capacity.

I want to continue that discussion here and rethink a neglected question in the current system: has hallucination been given the wrong kind of attention? Could it become a key to reconstructing the research paradigm?

## From AlphaGo to AI for Science

Human beings first began learning from AI, in a broad cultural sense, when AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol. That was already in 2016. In the years since, Go players have gradually moved from relying mainly on personal style and intuition to learning and interpreting AI moves. The 3-3 point invasion, once rarely used in the pre-AlphaGo era because it was considered easy to defend against but hard to develop, now appears in almost every game.

Looking back, that period feels distant and strangely suggestive. In a 2024 interview with The New York Times, Lee Sedol recalled:

> "Losing to AI, in a sense, meant my entire world collapsed. I could not adapt to it. I thought AI would one day defeat humans, but I did not think that day had arrived."

AI then became visible to everyone. Large models, protein-structure prediction, generative design, coding agents, and scientific copilots all made one thing clear: AI does not only automate known procedures. It can also expose unfamiliar solution spaces.

## Algorithm, Environment, and Prior

Reinforcement learning contains three important elements: the algorithm, the environment, and the prior. AlphaGo's success did not come from hallucination in the everyday sense. It came from a system that could explore possibilities within a well-defined environment and then test them rigorously.

## The Challenge of the Real World

Scientific research is different. The real world does not provide immediate reward signals. Biological systems are noisy. Experiments are slow. Mechanisms are incomplete. The environment is not a clean game board.

This is why hallucination in research is dangerous. A model may invent references, overgeneralize from weak evidence, or create plausible but false explanations. If the user treats such output as fact, the result is not creativity but error.

But the danger does not mean the phenomenon has no value. Scientific creativity often begins as a departure from inherited experience. The problem is how to connect departure with verification.

## The Experience Boundary of Large Language Models

Large language models learn from human text. Their experience boundary is shaped by the distribution of what has already been written. They are powerful at recombination and analogy, but they do not automatically know which recombination corresponds to reality.

When a model hallucinates, it sometimes crosses the boundary of documented experience. Most of the time, this is useless or wrong. Occasionally, however, the deviation may point toward a question that humans have not framed clearly enough.

The task is not to celebrate hallucination. It is to design systems that can separate creative variation from false assertion.

## Redefining Creative Hallucination

Creative hallucination should be understood as controlled hypothesis generation outside the normal range of prior experience. It becomes valuable only when paired with strict filtering.

In AI for Science, this means:

- hallucinated mechanisms must be checked against known biology;
- generated hypotheses must be connected to testable predictions;
- literature claims must be verified;
- experimental design must distinguish imagination from evidence;
- human scientists must decide which uncertainty is worth pursuing.

In this form, hallucination becomes closer to exploration than misinformation.

## The New Mission of Researchers

The researcher's role may therefore change. Instead of producing every hypothesis manually, scientists may increasingly design environments where AI can generate many possibilities and where verification systems can filter them. The scientist becomes a designer of search spaces, evidence standards, and intervention logic.

This is similar to what happened in Go. Human players did not become irrelevant after AlphaGo. They learned to interpret, test, and absorb AI's unfamiliar moves. In science, the challenge is harder because the rules are not closed. But the direction may be similar.

## Conclusion

Hallucination is not simply a defect to be eliminated, nor is it a magical source of creativity. It is a signal that generative systems can move beyond the surface of recorded experience. Without verification, this is dangerous. With verification, it may expand the boundary of research imagination.

The future of AI for Science will depend on whether we can build systems where creative variation and empirical discipline reinforce each other. Only then can hallucination become not an error to fear, but a raw material to be transformed into knowledge.
