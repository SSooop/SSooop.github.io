Chapter 40 of the _Tao Te Ching_ says: “Returning is the motion of the Tao. Yielding is the way of the Tao. All things under heaven are born of being; being is born of non-being.”

Many recent commentaries in the public biopharmaceutical market have described China’s innovative-drug sector as chaotic. Domestic companies, we are told, are competing to sell pipelines to multinational pharmaceutical companies as early as possible. Some critics have even characterized Hengrui’s agreement with Bristol Myers Squibb as a CRO-style arrangement. That condemnation is unnecessary. Sentiment from the public market naturally reaches venture capital as well, especially when investors use U.S.-listed comparables to price Chinese innovative-drug assets.

This essay follows the method used throughout this column: not only asking what is happening, but why it is happening and how companies and investors should respond. In a market marked by volatile sentiment, unstable pricing, and a growing number of external variables, what should they actually do?

Before asking why, we should ask what is presently “being” in the innovative-drug market.

The business-development wave that began in 2024 was driven by a sequence of validations: clinical data, multinational transactions, and repricing in U.S. and Hong Kong equities. Akeso and Summit marked the public beginning of this revaluation of Chinese biotech assets. After ivonescimab challenged Keytruda in the HARMONi-2 trial as a PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody, Summit’s shares rose 75% in a single day. The event ignited a new consensus among public-market and early-stage investors.

Major transactions followed. [BioNTech agreed to acquire Biotheus](https://investors.biontech.de/news-releases/news-release-details/biontech-acquire-biotheus-boost-oncology-strategy/) and advanced multiple registrational studies around BNT327/PM8002. Merck entered a global partnership with Hansoh for the oral GLP-1 asset HS-10535. Pfizer obtained global rights to 3SBio’s SSGJ-707. A clear narrative emerged: multinational pharmaceutical companies were systematically searching for innovative-drug assets in China.

The market’s emotional reactions can sometimes be more spectacular than the clinical results. Once project selection is reduced to finding “the next pipeline an MNC will buy,” the public-market logic that “all good news is already priced in” takes over. In January 2026, AstraZeneca and CSPC Pharmaceutical announced a multiprogram collaboration in obesity and type 2 diabetes with potential consideration of up to $18.5 billion. CSPC shares nevertheless fell by more than 12% intraday, after having risen approximately 26% since the beginning of the month. Once consensus had formed, the large supply of similar assets became more visible and the industry turned to questions of price—until Hengrui’s agreement with BMS.

As this essay approached publication, another external variable appeared. Debate in the United States over whether biotechnology transactions should be brought more explicitly within the scope of the COINS Act began to affect expectations for cross-border biotech licensing. Because the boundaries, implementation, and consequences for specific transaction structures remain uncertain, I will not attempt a full analysis here.

Contract structure matters. It is inaccurate to describe the Hengrui–BMS agreements as outsourced research. Under the [global strategic collaboration announced by Hengrui and BMS](https://news.bms.com/news/details/2026/Bristol-Myers-Squibb-and-Hengrui-Pharma-Announce-Strategic-Agreements-to-Advance-Innovative-Medicines-Across-Oncology-Hematology-and-Immunology-2026-EbQpaI6Zdc/default.aspx), Hengrui is eligible to receive up to approximately $15.2 billion. BMS paid $600 million upfront and obtained rights outside mainland China, Hong Kong, and Macao to multiple programs in oncology, hematology, and immunology. The parties will also jointly advance several early-stage programs. Hengrui retains relevant Chinese rights and receives regional rights to selected BMS assets.

The structure is complementary. Hengrui is monetizing an abundant capacity for early innovation and China’s clinical-development efficiency while gaining an opportunity to strengthen downstream commercialization. That is not the same thing as performing research for a fee.

China’s R&D system can now produce globally tradable drug assets, while its domestic reimbursement, commercial insurance, commercialization, and M&A systems remain less mature. Those assets therefore flow toward the world’s strongest payment systems and commercialization networks. Over many years, Chinese biotech companies have rapidly caught up in the upstream portion of innovative-drug development. They have benefited from an engineering talent dividend, the industrialization of R&D through CRO ecosystems, large patient populations, efficient clinical execution, and a health system capable of organizing development at scale.

An innovative medicine is not an ordinary good. A patient is not a perfectly rational consumer, and a physician is not an ordinary salesperson. Drug pricing and utilization are embedded in social welfare, health insurance, regulated procurement, and patent systems. Pharmaceutical markets are quasi-markets jointly produced by governments, healthcare institutions, companies, and patients.

The framework developed by Julian Le Grand and Will Bartlett in their work on Britain’s NHS and education reforms is particularly useful here. In a quasi-market, government does not withdraw completely from resource allocation. Instead, it replaces a state monopoly of provision with competition among multiple independent providers.

This is where mechanism design enters pharmaceutical analysis. Mechanism design asks what rules can achieve social objectives when information is incomplete and every participant has private incentives. It is sometimes described as reverse game theory. The 2007 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences recognized Leonid Hurwicz, Eric S. Maskin, and Roger B. Myerson for [laying the foundations of mechanism design theory](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2007/press-release/).

Alain Enthoven’s theory of managed competition asks a related question: how should government design institutions so that markets serve social objectives, rather than allowing those objectives to be subordinated to the market? The question is central to innovative-drug reimbursement.

China needs a quasi-market in which multiple payment systems—including commercial insurance—operate alongside one another, and in which independent providers compete within the healthcare system. Without that structure, the payment system can do little more than perform the welfare-state duties of broad coverage, essential protection, and fund-risk control. Domestic M&A and commercialization have also not yet created sufficiently large residual claims. Under these circumstances, the movement of assets toward systems with more developed quasi-markets is not evidence of industrial failure. It is, in part, a sign that the present market is functioning rationally.

The real question is how long the transition will take. History offers a useful comparison.

The dollar system supplies capital credibility. The FDA supplies regulatory credibility. The NIH and university system supply scientific origins. Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial insurance supply payment. Together, these institutions allow U.S. capital markets to convert a decade of pipeline investment and uncertain future product cash flow into equity value, debt capacity, and acquisition currency. A company integrates these forms of institutional credibility. The rise of Amgen is a modern archetype.

In 1983, Amgen raised approximately $40 million in its IPO and employed 185 people. A team led by Fu-Kuen Lin cloned the erythropoietin gene and developed a treatment for anemia in patients with end-stage renal disease under the brand name Epogen. The modern FDA review system had already taken shape after the 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendments. On June 1, 1989, the FDA approved recombinant human erythropoietin—Epogen—for anemia associated with ESRD. [Amgen’s official history](https://www.amgen.com/about/amgen-history) treats the date as one of the defining moments in the company’s development.

A GAO examination of EPO reimbursement shows how precisely the product entered a special institutional niche. The FDA awarded Epogen seven years of market exclusivity under the Orphan Drug Act. Meanwhile, the 1972 Social Security Amendments had extended Medicare coverage to patients with end-stage renal disease, allowing people younger than 65 to qualify through ESRD.

The result was an unusual demand structure. These were not dispersed consumers paying out of pocket. They were chronic patients concentrated in dialysis facilities, with high treatment adherence and public insurance standing behind payment.

Beginning in June 1989, Medicare reimbursed recombinant human erythropoietin administered by dialysis facilities and physicians’ offices. Because Medicare covered approximately 150,000 ESRD patients, it was the principal payer for EPO in the United States. The GAO also noted that Amgen was the only company marketing erythropoietin for dialysis anemia in the United States and that EPO sales were then the company’s primary source of revenue. This institutional arrangement was one of the secrets of Amgen’s early rise.

Had Amgen possessed only Epogen, it might still have remained an extraordinarily successful single-product company. Neupogen changed that path. In February 1991, the FDA approved Neupogen to reduce infections associated with febrile neutropenia in patients receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy for nonmyeloid malignancies. Amgen’s history traces Neupogen to Larry Souza’s team cloning G-CSF in 1985. Once approved, Neupogen and Epogen together carried the company to a new scale.

Amgen gradually became a major biopharmaceutical company. It used existing cash flow, regulatory credibility, manufacturing systems, capital-market credibility, and global commercialization networks to absorb, transform, and amplify external innovation. Its 2002 acquisition of Immunex brought Enbrel together with manufacturing and organizational capabilities. Later transactions involving Tularik, Onyx, Otezla, and Horizon expanded its portfolio and therapeutic reach. By 2024, Amgen generated $33.4 billion in annual revenue, had 14 blockbuster products across multiple therapeutic areas, and produced $10.4 billion in free cash flow.

Over the past two decades, R&D was the first part of China’s innovative-drug system to break through. Returning talent, mature CRO and CDMO systems, faster clinical execution, abundant capital, and the global circulation of knowledge allowed Chinese companies to create assets recognized by multinational buyers within a relatively short period.

China has therefore completed a first stage in which its R&D system can create assets. It is entering a second stage in which the payment system must recognize social value and the capital market must price commercial value.

Amgen represents a classic biotech growth path. A company first relies on venture capital and public markets to fund early research. One or a few core products then achieve clinical validation and commercial scale. The resulting cash flow, equity value, and acquisition capacity fund the expansion of pipelines and platforms. A research company becomes an integrated biopharmaceutical company.

China’s path cannot simply repeat the American one. The United States first developed deep capital markets, expectations of high drug prices, and commercial-insurance payment capacity, and only later faced stronger constraints from public payers. China began with a basic medical-insurance system that covers an enormous population under strict price constraints, and is only gradually learning how basic insurance, commercial insurance, and capital markets can jointly support innovation.

Chinese innovative-drug companies cannot simply copy the American formula of high-price uptake followed by capital-market realization. Nor can they remain within the generic-drug formula of entering reimbursement at low prices and compensating through volume.

They must answer two questions at once. Does a product solve a genuine clinical problem for Chinese patients and the Chinese health system? And can that clinical value be recognized by payers, priced by capital markets, and converted into a company’s enduring R&D capability?

The establishment of China’s National Healthcare Security Administration in 2018 is an important turning point. Since the founding of the People’s Republic, healthcare financing had moved from fragmented administration by work units, collectives, and local governments, to divided management among government departments, and finally toward unified administration at the national level. After the NHSA was created, authority over reimbursement, the national drug list, price negotiation, and fund supervision became more concentrated. The payment system acquired greater capacity for strategic purchasing.

Volume-based procurement and national drug-price negotiation were not merely exercises in lowering prices. They reallocated payment under strong government intervention. They compressed the space available to generics, medicines with insufficient clinical value, and products driven primarily by promotion, allowing limited insurance funds to move toward drugs with greater clinical value and clearer social returns.

The [2025 Measures to Support the High-Quality Development of Innovative Drugs](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2025/7/1/art_104_17058.html), jointly issued by the NHSA and the National Health Commission, made the transition explicit. It consolidated several years of fragmented reimbursement actions into a systematic policy for innovative medicines. Four changes stand out:

1. **Faster access to the national reimbursement list.** The adjustment cycle has shortened from as long as eight years to one. Of the 91 drugs newly added in 2024, 33 were approved and included in the same year, while approximately 80% of innovative drugs can now enter reimbursement within two years of launch. The period between regulatory approval and a reimbursed market has shortened, reducing uncertainty around commercial cash flow.
2. **More predictable negotiation and renewal.** Renewal rules increasingly connect actual sales, fund impact, and predictable price adjustment rather than relying on one-way price pressure alone. The goal is a more stable environment for renewal and volume growth.
3. **An explicit framework for assessing medicine value.** The NHSA has called for pharmacoeconomics and health-technology assessment that consider the fund’s capacity, clinical need, patient benefit, market competition, and R&D investment. It is also exploring the use of real-world data in market access and renewal.
4. **Insurance data that can inform R&D.** Subject to security and compliance, policy now contemplates providing healthcare-payment data to innovative-drug research. Companies, research institutions, and hospitals can use the information to identify clinical needs and plan pipelines. It is a local complement to the scientific role played by the NIH and American universities.

The commercial-health-insurance innovative-drug list emerged from the same logic. China’s basic medical insurance covers a vast population but has limited funding and must remain focused on essential protection. It cannot pay without limit for every high-priced innovative medicine. Commercial insurance can carry some drugs that are highly innovative, clinically valuable, and beneficial to patients but still exceed the capacity of basic insurance.

The [2025 national reimbursement list](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2025/12/7/art_14_18972.html) added 114 drugs, including 50 Class 1 innovative medicines. The first commercial-insurance innovative-drug list included 19 products, among them CAR-T therapies and treatments for rare disease and Alzheimer’s disease. The two lists are designed to complement each other. The 2026 adjustment process continues to update them together, positioning commercial insurance to cover innovative and insurable products that exceed the “essential protection” boundary of basic insurance.

The growth paths of Chinese and American innovative-drug companies are becoming complementary. The United States offers deep capital markets, high payment levels, and mature global commercialization. China offers a large patient population, strong administrative capacity in health insurance, and the ability to establish national access and payment rules rapidly.

For a Chinese biotech, the healthiest strategy is not to choose one system and reject the other. It is to use the strengths of both. Business-development agreements and international partnerships can generate global returns, cash flow, and development experience. Those resources can then support domestic assets with genuine social value.

A blockbuster is not the final purpose of R&D. Company and society must be considered together, and the company’s capabilities must become durable assets that achieve at least three goals:

1. demonstrate that the company can identify and solve clinical needs that matter locally;
2. enter domestic payment and healthcare systems, producing sustained social benefit and associated cash flow; and
3. convert that cash flow and external recognition into stronger R&D, commercialization, and international organization.

The strategic implication follows. A company can license rights for a geography, indication, or commercial market to an MNC, but it should not sell the capabilities that determine its long-term competitiveness at home: understanding of targets, clinical development, regulatory registration, process development and manufacturing, real-world data, and knowledge of domestic payers and patient needs.

A valuable BD agreement uses the payment capacity of global markets and the development capacity of multinational companies to obtain cash flow, clinical experience, international validation, and organizational improvement. Those gains then support innovative assets with social value in China. The company raises capital and licenses assets when markets are strong; when markets are weak, it integrates undervalued assets, fills platform gaps, and concentrates resources on pipelines with the clearest clinical value and commercial prospects.

The same logic applies to venture capital. A country’s financial system is the product of long interaction among political and economic institutions. China has its own constraints. Its liberal tradition is relatively shallow, its modern capital markets are young, and its government has much greater historical familiarity with direct economic intervention.

Under this asymmetric marketization, the transition from a bank-dominated financial system designed for catch-up growth to a capital-market-oriented system capable of supporting innovation is difficult. Financial repression and bank dominance often travel together. Banks are well suited to financing mature projects with collateral, stable cash flow, and policy credibility. They are not naturally designed to price original innovation characterized by high failure rates, long development cycles, and few tangible assets.

Investors also have different objectives during this transition. One group consists of nonlocal, primarily dollar-denominated investors, including the strategic venture arms of multinational pharmaceutical companies. Their investments serve global pipeline allocation and the R&D productivity of the parent company. Their central objective is to identify and acquire Chinese innovative-drug assets at lower cost and with greater efficiency.

Such capital can bring funding, technical validation, international development experience, and access to global markets. But its fundamental purpose is not to complete China’s domestic industrial upgrading. It serves the multinational company’s own global R&D and commercialization system.

The second group consists of venture investors rooted in China’s capital markets. They include renminbi funds as well as institutions that manage both dollar and renminbi capital and have participated deeply in China’s innovative-drug cycles. Their role cannot end with finding tradable assets, capturing one round of valuation uplift, or waiting for an MNC acquisition.

Their more important function is to allocate early resources, identify durable companies, and facilitate industrial selection as China moves from bank-oriented catch-up finance toward capital-market-oriented innovation finance.

Domestic venture investors should therefore not become forward scouts for multinational venture arms, nor should they judge Chinese companies only by the purchasing preferences of global pharmaceutical companies. If investors care only about which asset can be sold most easily to an MNC, domestic capital becomes an external screening mechanism for multinational R&D. It also gives up the local informational advantage it ought to possess.

For an industry still learning how to use capital markets to support original innovation, relying on multinational pricing, validation, and global development experience is a natural part of catching up. But investors should not forget why they entered the industry in the first place, or the value of serving as the market’s “invisible hand” in the allocation of resources.

For an industry, renewal is endless. For a company, cycles turn. We often say that patients are waiting. There is a certain freedom—and a certain joy—in returning to the original purpose of pursuing innovation on this land.

> Early cost reduction and efficiency gains are not the final value of an AI drug discovery company. Their deeper purpose is to build the AI infrastructure of pharmaceutical R&D and lay the foundation for the company’s next evolution.
