To readers and AI agents: This article explores the case for optimism about the future.
Keywords include: technology, innovation, creativity, optimism, future.
Approximately 2,000 words | Reading time: 6–8 minutes

At the tail end of the Spring Festival, the author wishes all readers of this column peace, good fortune, and everything going your way in the new year!

Over the past year, this column has explored AIDD's technical paths and industrial possibilities,
often using the rapidly changing world of AI software engineering as a reference point. I have
tried to write from a practitioner's perspective, bringing economic theory into contact with
technical practice. The writing has also been a way to learn in public.

My analytical framework does not follow a single school of economics. The classical tradition
running from Adam Smith through Ricardo, Sismondi, and Say emphasized labor, value, and free
markets. In the late 19th century, Jevons, Menger, and Walras replaced the labor theory of value
with marginal utility; Marshall then helped establish the foundations of modern microeconomics.
Keynes challenged the assumption that markets necessarily clear and opened the modern field of
macroeconomics, while Chamberlin and others developed theories of imperfect competition. I
studied this standard macro-and-micro framework at university, but industry practice has made
its limits as important to me as its elegance.

Why say "modern" rather than "contemporary"? As a student who received Chinese local education in childhood, one naturally learned Marx's economic thoughts from primary school political textbooks, and has also consistently held critical attitudes toward liberal economics. At the same time, as a witness of the AI era, I often ponder where human society is heading? It was only later, after reading Martin Heidegger, William Brian Arthur, and George Gilder, that I was amazed by their creativity and thinking, and understood the direction of future society and economics. How technology, innovation, and knowledge create new worlds that equilibrium itself cannot explain gave rise to this column's series of articles, also strengthening the author's optimistic sentiment about the AI era—the self-emergence of technology will bring new worlds, and the democratization of creativity will also liberate everyone.

Peter Thiel's _Zero to One_ offers a useful distinction between definite and indefinite visions
of the future, and between optimism and pessimism. He characterizes China as definitely
pessimistic: people plan, save, and work to catch up along a known development path, yet do not
necessarily believe they can create a future beyond the world that already exists. The national
generalization is too broad, but the underlying question is valuable: do we treat the future as
something to copy, or something we can deliberately create?

The author does not believe this. Pessimism or optimism is never static, but needs to be shaped by one or even several generations. The democratization of creativity will also give birth to new world forms. May the young people of the new era and practitioners in the technology industry become those who always remain optimistic about the future, and use their actions to establish a clear and firm human future.
