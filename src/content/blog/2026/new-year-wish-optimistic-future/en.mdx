At the tail end of the Spring Festival, the author wishes all readers of this column peace, good fortune, and everything going your way in the new year!

Over the past year, this column has discussed AIDD's technological paths and industrial visions, using AI + software engineering—the fastest-developing field—as a reference template. It attempts to stand from the perspective of industry practitioners, combining economic theory analysis with technological practice. The author has also been continuously learning through writing and has gained a lot.

From the theoretical framework perspective, the author has not completely followed the classical economics analysis path. The systematic development of the economic system can be traced back to Adam Smith, promoted through David Ricardo, Sismondi, Jean-Baptiste Say, and others, gradually forming the core system of Classical Economics, emphasizing labor theory of value and free market thinking. In the late 19th century, Jevons, Menger, and Walras initiated the "Marginal Revolution," replacing labor theory of value with marginal utility analysis, and economics entered the Neoclassical Economics era. Marshall built upon this, laying the foundation for the basic framework of modern microeconomics. After the 20th century, the Keynesian Revolution fundamentally challenged the neoclassical assumption that markets can self-clear, giving birth to the brand-new field of macroeconomics; Chamberlin and others introduced imperfect competition theory at the micro level, revising the idealized assumption of perfect competition. During university, the author also systematically studied the classic textbook "Principles of Economics" according to the curriculum, thereby mastering the "modern" framework of macro and microeconomics.

Why say "modern" rather than "contemporary"? As a student who received Chinese local education in childhood, one naturally learned Marx's economic thoughts from primary school political textbooks, and has also consistently held critical attitudes toward liberal economics. At the same time, as a witness of the AI era, I often ponder where human society is heading? It was only later, after reading Martin Heidegger, William Brian Arthur, and George Gilder, that I was amazed by their creativity and thinking, and understood the direction of future society and economics. How technology, innovation, and knowledge create new worlds that equilibrium itself cannot explain gave rise to this column's series of articles, also strengthening the author's optimistic sentiment about the AI era—the self-emergence of technology will bring new worlds, and the democratization of creativity will also liberate everyone.

Finally, borrowing from Peter Thiel's discussion in 《From Zero to One》 about definite vs. indefinite views of the future and optimistic vs. pessimistic attitudes to conclude. He says the Chinese have definite expectations for the future, but this expectation is based on imagining replicating today's American development path: therefore they save desperately and strive to catch up, but do not believe they can create something that surpasses the existing world, so the Chinese are definite pessimists about the future.

The author does not believe this. Pessimism or optimism is never static, but needs to be shaped by one or even several generations. The democratization of creativity will also give birth to new world forms. May the young people of the new era and practitioners in the technology industry become those who always remain optimistic about the future, and use their actions to establish a clear and firm human future.
