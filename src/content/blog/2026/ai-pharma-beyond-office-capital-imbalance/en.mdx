It has been a while since my last update. Part of the reason is that there is not always something worth writing. Another is that deep, long-form text does poorly on content platforms governed by recommendation algorithms. Too many essays are written, published, consumed at speed, and then disappear. That feels like a waste. I may publish less often from now on, aiming instead for higher-quality and more interesting work, while experimenting with new platforms and formats so these essays can remain accessible over the long term.

What prompted this essay was the product strategy and capital maneuvering of Anthropic and OpenAI as they navigate enormous financing rounds, enterprise monetization, and expectations of an eventual public listing. Those developments also cast AI drug discovery in a revealing light.

In the spring of 2026, Silicon Valley’s AI sector carried an anxious mixture of “one last celebration” and “the night before the IPO.”

On April 7, Anthropic made [Claude Mythos Preview](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mythos-preview) available to a limited group of trusted partners through Project Glasswing. It was not a single-purpose “defense model” released to ordinary users, but a restricted-access, general-purpose model with unusually strong cybersecurity capabilities. An evaluation by the UK [AI Security Institute](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities) found that Mythos Preview was the first model to complete “The Last Ones,” a 32-step simulated corporate-network attack. It succeeded end to end in three of ten attempts and completed an average of 22 steps.

On April 17, Anthropic released [Claude Design](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-design-anthropic-labs), which can produce prototypes, presentations, and one-page materials from natural-language instructions, codebases, and design assets. Markets quickly interpreted it as a direct challenge to workflows built around Figma, Canva, and adjacent design tools.

OpenAI was moving into specialized capabilities at a similar pace. On April 14 it introduced [GPT‑5.4‑Cyber](https://openai.com/index/scaling-trusted-access-for-cyber-defense/) for trusted defenders. On April 16 it launched [GPT‑Rosalind](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-rosalind/) for life-sciences research, emphasizing biological reasoning, evidence synthesis, experimental planning, and workflows spanning scientific tools. Cybersecurity, design, and life sciences may appear to be very different domains, but these products share a practical commercial direction: place frontier models inside concrete workflows whose boundaries are clear, whose value can be measured, and which can be sold to enterprises.

As the AI leaders strengthen their capital-market narratives, the concentration of releases also serves as evidence of their ability to monetize. Yet it raises a question. Why do startups that gather some of humanity’s most capable minds—and companies with unmatched resources, sitting at the center of the AGI narrative—compete so intensely over such a small part of social production?

The answer is now fairly obvious: these tasks are highly digitized, have short feedback loops, and are relatively easy to deliver. Rather than repeat that familiar observation, this essay asks a deeper set of questions. Why is the pattern so stable? What theoretical structure sustains it? And if AI is to enter the messier worlds of pharmaceuticals, manufacturing, energy, medicine, and agriculture, where should the breakthrough begin?

We first need a clearer conceptual boundary. Let us call the part of the “office economy” in which AI currently thrives a digitally closed space: inputs, computation, and outputs can all remain inside existing computing systems, without depending on complicated physical feedback or large-scale organizational reconstruction.

Some forms of software engineering, especially development tasks, are archetypal digitally closed work. Code can be committed to a repository; tests can run in CI; deployment can happen in the cloud; logs can be collected automatically; errors can be reproduced; regressions can be measured. Together, these conditions explain the extraordinary growth of agentic coding.

This description is necessarily time-dependent. As technology improves and resources move among industries in response to new demand, tomorrow’s computing systems will widen the boundaries of what today’s systems can contain. OpenAI’s acquisition of Astral, discussed in a previous essay, is one concrete example of expanding the capabilities of the computational substrate itself.

Yet not everything that appears to happen on a computer is digitally closed. Building a breakout consumer app—even the most conventional internet application—still requires crossing the screen. Consider Meituan. Its code, interfaces, and recommendation algorithms run on servers, but its success depends on user attention, distribution, brand, local fulfillment, regulatory relationships, supply-chain capability, and organizational execution. The algorithm is only one component, and often not the hardest one.

This distinction is particularly important for AI drug discovery. Drug development is not software engineering at a larger scale. It is a physically and organizationally open system. We can therefore state a second definition:

> A physically and organizationally open system is one in which outcomes must survive the dual tests of the physical world and social organization.

Molecular properties must be measured in real experiments. Efficacy and toxicity must be tested, layer by layer, in cells, animals, human beings, and real-world data. At the same time, protocol design, ethics review, CRO coordination, patient recruitment, indication selection, payer acceptance, regulatory communication, and patent contracts form an equally complicated organizational process.

I strongly recommend a series of practical evaluations of medical LLMs published by the WeChat account “稳定的坤.” They also help explain the success of products such as OpenEvidence. Clinicians do not need an oracle that spends every day diagnosing exotic medical mysteries, because most daily medicine is not made up of exotic mysteries. A useful product embeds itself in the information flows clinicians confront every day: locating guidelines, reading papers, assessing evidence, drafting notes, forming differential diagnoses, and communicating with patients.

For many hospitals, spending the same budget to replace aging computers and eliminate daily lag might be more useful than building an “AI diagnostic system” detached from clinical workflow. The example sounds like a joke, but it captures the enormous distance between technical capability and realized productivity.

Technical capability does not automatically become productivity. Theories of general-purpose technologies (GPTs) were developed in part to explain precisely this problem. Against the historical experience of electrification and the information-technology productivity paradox of the 1980s and 1990s, scholars including Paul David, Timothy Bresnahan, Manuel Trajtenberg, Erik Brynjolfsson, and Daniel Rock argued from different directions that even widely diffused technology may take time to appear in productivity statistics. Its effect is constrained by complementary investment and the long process of reconstructing systems around it.

A point technology must trigger a broad set of complementary innovations before it can become a social return. Electricity, computers, the internet, and AI all share this property. Their full return comes not only from the technology itself but from redesigned processes, organizations, skills, infrastructure, and commercial institutions.

For clarity, we can compress the results of these different theories into a two-stage heuristic:

$$
P_{\mathrm{social}}(t)=G(t)-C(t)
$$

Here, $G(t)$ is the technology’s social-benefit function. It commonly follows an S-shaped diffusion pattern: growth is slow at first because use cases, infrastructure, and complementary capabilities are scarce, then accelerates as the technology matures and diffuses.

$C(t)$ is the cost of reconstructing the surrounding system, and it is central to the current AI impasse. It can be very high at the beginning of a transition, comprising investment in intangible assets such as process redesign, workforce renewal, data governance, infrastructure upgrades, and the hidden losses created when familiar systems are disrupted. Only as reconstruction progresses does its marginal cost begin to taper.

The difference between these functions produces the familiar productivity J-curve. Technical capability arrives, but organizational productivity initially falls. The return on complementary investment becomes visible only after the reconstruction phase has been crossed.

Why, then, are so few actors willing to absorb $C(t)$ on the left side of the J-curve? If everyone expects reconstruction eventually to unlock explosive growth in $G(t)$, rational capital should invest in the trough, accepting early losses for long-run profit. In practice, however, AI investment has intensified rather than resolved the problem.

From the perspective of an individual company or investor, we can write a second simplified function:

$$
R_{\mathrm{private}}(t)=\alpha G(t)+R(t)-C(t)-K(t)
$$

$\alpha G(t)$ is the private share of the social benefit the company helps create, with $\alpha$ representing the appropriability coefficient. $R(t)$ is platform rent, gateway rent, and other forms of lock-in. $C(t)$ remains the cost of reconstructing the organization. $K(t)$ is the duplicated, race-like capital expenditure incurred in pursuit of a privileged future position.

The structural mismatch is now visible. Private returns turn positive only when the portion of social value the firm can capture, plus its platform rents, covers reconstruction costs and competitive investment. Society needs $C(t)$ to fall in reality and $G(t)$ to rise in reality. Capital seeks to maximize $\alpha$ and $R(t)$.

This is the distribution problem in the diffusion of a general-purpose technology. David Teece long ago argued that whether an innovator profits depends on the appropriability regime, complementary assets, and dominant design—not simply on whether the technology is advanced. When complementary assets belong to someone else, benefits are difficult to exclude, and organizational reconstruction is expensive, private capital tends to avoid the reconstruction the system actually needs.

Today’s AI investment logic displays three related distortions:

- **Appropriability distortion:** The investments most likely to raise social productivity are often open, foundational, and sector-wide: data standards, process redesign, experimental infrastructure, regulatory interfaces, organizational training, and cross-institution coordination. Their benefits spill over and are difficult for one company to capture. Capital therefore prefers components with clear private boundaries—model APIs, enterprise accounts, proprietary data, closed plugins, workflow lock-in, and permission systems.
- **Platform-rent distortion:** The internet era’s most successful business model aggregates multiple groups on one system, then collects long-run rent through network effects, switching costs, and rule-setting power. Rochet and Tirole’s work on two-sided markets emphasizes a platform’s ability to attract both sides and capture aggregate value through pricing and governance. That became the financing narrative most familiar to internet investors backing early AI startups.
- **Race-redundancy distortion:** When a market is imagined as winner-take-most, rational companies duplicate immense fixed costs simply to avoid exclusion from the future platform structure. Multiple AI firms train similar models, buy GPUs, build data centers, pursue the same enterprise customers, and tell similar agent stories. Each decision is rational at the firm level because losing the platform race could reduce future rents to zero. At the social level, much of the spending may still be duplicative. This is why FOMO dominated investor sentiment throughout 2025.

AI drug discovery, however, is not a gateway business of this kind.

Whether it is called AIDD or CADD, the business remains pharmaceuticals. Unless the foundations of regulation, insurance reimbursement, and government–industry price negotiation change, the profits of innovative-drug companies will continue to come mainly from a small number of successful assets with high sales, pricing power, and quasi-monopoly protection during the patent term.

The discovery layer of AI pharma therefore cannot build an internet-platform business by “owning the gateway, aggregating traffic, and extracting rent.” Many AI drug-discovery platforms appear to occupy an industry gateway but are actually located at a false gateway. They reduce friction for other companies, improve early discovery, and save part of the cost of failure—then receive only a thin share of the economics. By contrast, the production layer may be capable of producing a large CRDMO such as WuXi AppTec, because scarce complementary assets can support much stronger rent capture.

If platform rent $R(t)$ is difficult to obtain, can an AI company raise its appropriability coefficient $\alpha$? General AI companies and large financial institutions have offered one aggressive answer.

On May 4, Anthropic announced a new [enterprise AI services company](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-ai-services-company) with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs. The organization targets mid-sized companies; Anthropic’s applied AI engineers and the new firm’s engineering teams will jointly identify opportunities, build custom systems, and support customers over time. The same day, the media reported [OpenAI’s plans for an enterprise AI services joint venture with several large private-equity institutions](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-04/openai-finalizes-10-billion-joint-venture-with-pe-firms-to-deploy-ai).

The structure can be summarized as “PE-JV + FDE.” Private-equity firms contribute capital and a large network of portfolio companies. AI firms contribute models and forward-deployed engineers. The new entity brings AI into the core processes of those portfolio companies. The objective is not merely to sell more seats; it is to shorten the deployment chain, participate directly in organizational reconstruction, and improve the appropriability of the value created.

Why not rely exclusively on Accenture, Deloitte, and other firms that already have customer relationships and implementation capacity? Anthropic’s announcement did not reject system integrators; it explicitly said the company would continue investing in those partnerships. The distinctive value of a PE joint venture is that it creates an additional distribution channel with some of the properties of an internal market:

- A private-equity firm is itself an enormous customer aggregator, with portfolio companies forming a ready deployment network.
- It can own part of the service company while using AI to improve portfolio-company earnings and exit valuations, bringing the incentives into closer alignment.
- The biggest obstacle to AI deployment in a mid-sized company is often not acquiring a model. It is persuading management, completing procurement, redesigning processes, and accepting transition risk. Direct pressure from a controlling shareholder can dramatically compress a cycle that would otherwise take months.

Enterprise AI can treat a portfolio as a captive distribution channel. AI drug discovery faces something very different: not a digital workflow that FDEs can quickly rewrite, but a physical and organizational validation chain made of wet-lab experiments, clinical trials, ethics review, regulatory communication, reimbursement, manufacturing scale-up, and patent contracts.

If AI pharma wants to increase $\alpha$, it cannot remain only a software platform for the discovery layer. It may not even be enough to retain the broader identity of an “R&D platform.” It must bind itself more deeply to drug assets, experimental capacity, control of clinical development, regulatory capability, and manufacturing delivery.

The ultimate direction of AI drug discovery is therefore to become a real pharmaceutical company—perhaps even a Big Pharma. Otherwise, it can capture neither the internet platform’s $R(t)$ nor enough of the $\alpha G(t)$ created by an innovative drug. It will remain in the most expensive value chain in the economy, earning service margins for the owners of the assets and channels. That was also the fate confronting countless biotech companies before the AI era.
