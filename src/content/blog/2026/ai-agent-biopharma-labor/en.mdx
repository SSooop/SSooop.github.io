("Calculating Clerks" in the mid-19th century)

In the mid-19th century, behind the busy bank counters in London, a group known as "Calculating Clerks" worked at their desks day after day. They had no screens, only stacks of ledgers, dip pens, and mechanical computing devices called Arithmometers. Under dim gaslight, thousands of numbers were precisely transcribed, summed, and verified. From a brain metabolism perspective, the load carried by their constantly high-frequency firing cerebral cortex would be quite heavy even by today's standards. However, within the social cognitive framework of the time, this was only defined as a "sedentary manual labor."

The Greek philosopher Aristotle was the first to focus on the human "hierarchy of action" framework. In his classical ethics work The Nicomachean Ethics, he divided human activities into: production aimed at products (Poiesis / Techne), practice aimed at action itself based on rational choice (Praxis), and Phronesis—the highest intellectual virtue that dominates practice. Choice (Prohairesis) is the key link connecting desire and reason, enabling practice to begin. When this classical framework is projected onto 19th-century bank counters, its hierarchical division remains clear.

The bottom layer of execution (How / Techne) concerns how to neatly write numbers and quickly operate machines. This was considered a core skill at the time, but essentially, it was closer to a form of "biological analog computation." Once rules are determined, they have the highest degree of formalization and are therefore most easily replaced by mechanical logic.

The slightly higher layer of choice (Which / Prohairesis) involves logical branching and judgment. Facing a complex international remittance, which account template should be applied—this requires matching based on experience. However, under established goal constraints, this still belongs to closed-loop thinking.

And at the top layer of definition (What & Why / Phronesis) is the only area that cannot be fully formalized. When a poorly dressed but determined young man walks into a bank to apply for a loan, the existing rulebook becomes invalid—the bank employee needs to redefine "value" and "risk."

When we look back at our current daily work, perhaps we are often interspersed among these three layers. Among the routine process execution and immediate response busyness, how often do we truly触及 (touch upon) moments of "defining problems"? This is probably a proposition worth pondering after endless paperwork.

(Manus siding with Meta)

This re-examination of "mental labor" did not originate from a single technological breakthrough, but was pushed to the forefront in a series of changes at the beginning of 2026. On December 30, 2025, Meta announced the acquisition of Manus through full equity, clearly moving general-purpose agents out of the context of geek experiments and tool-based applications, beginning to be regarded as a digital infrastructure that can be systematically absorbed by large organizations. A few weeks later, Anthropic released Claude for Health, further embedding Agents directly into bioinformatics—a professional field long considered to have high entry barriers—on the basis of its previous life science version, thus triggering re-examination of daily work by some analysis positions within multinational pharmaceutical companies (MNCs).

If the 19th-century arithmetic calculator (Arithmometer) accomplished the剥离 (stripping) of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division from the human brain, then the current wave of Agents is more like a "demystification" process targeting the internal structure of intellectual labor. Bioinformatics has long been regarded as a typical "high-barrier mental black box," but an important source of its complexity does not entirely come from the problem itself, but from the sustained, systematic imbalance in understanding the intermediate process between upstream and downstream. In large organizations, experimental-side biologists often don't clearly understand what bioinformatics scientists on the analysis side are doing—this cognitive gap, amplified by interdisciplinary and organizational division of labor, wraps a lot of highly repetitive parameter adjustment, process building, and result organization in unnecessary professional mystique. Agents like Claude for Health make business processes originally implicit in personal experience and oral collaboration explicit, and reorganize fragmented operation paths into reusable Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) or standardized Workflows. This impact itself does not equal certain positions being "replaced"—more accurately, this change exposes: which work content never depended on genuine expert judgment (phronesis) from the start.

The "demystification" brought by Agents does not mean they can immediately rise to become decision-making centers within MNCs. On the contrary, in actual R&D practice, computational analysis has long occupied a relatively restrained position. Whether from technical practice itself or from practitioners' subjective experiences, it is not difficult to observe:

> The work of bioinformatics scientists in pharmaceutical companies is often overlooked by biologists, and the work they do is always supportive work... Biologists would rather refer to one or two papers or limited experimental results than believe in multi-omics big data analysis results.
> https://www.reddit.com/r/bioinformatics/comments/zuxkst/how_can_the_work_of_bioinformatics_scientists_in/?rdt=55843

As innovative drug R&D gradually enters the post-"Low-Hanging Fruit" depletion phase, linear development paths around validated targets are converging. Simpler logic structures can no longer sustain continuous innovative output—R&D value is beginning to concentrate on more complex systems biology problems. Such problems are often driven by heterogeneous data, and their breakthrough starting points still highly depend on biological insights into new targets or new mechanisms of action (MOA). As the space of fully validatable targets gradually shrinks, the innovation focus is shifting toward more complex and less formalizable new MOAs. In this process, bioinformatics more undertakes the role of verification and explanation rather than a priori prophecy of results.

In some frequently cited success cases, the key role of computation often occurs in the latter half of the timeline. A representative example is the 2016 study by Scott et al. published in Science Translational Medicine, which analyzed the association between GLP1R missense mutation rs10305492 and reduced fasting blood glucose, decreased type 2 diabetes risk, and cardiovascular protection. Against the backdrop of the FDA requiring all new hypoglycemic drugs to demonstrate cardiovascular safety, this population genetics study provided important biological plausibility a priori evidence for subsequent GLP-1 agonist CVOTs and had a sustained impact on regulatory attitude evolution.

> Our results provide an encouraging signal that these agents may be associated with benefit, a question currently being addressed in randomized controlled trials.
> https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.aad3744

This "post-hoc confirmation"-centric work mode is often rigorous in statistical terms, but its scope of activity is always confined within established rules: finding supporting evidence within a defined problem space. This constitutes the daily state of most MNC internal bioinformatics teams—providing structured endorsement of experimental conclusions with data. In contrast, true a priori prediction and algorithm-driven drug R&D still highly depend on deeper understanding of biological essence, and its realization path necessarily involves technological and cognitive evolution over longer time scales, making it difficult to be completed solely by scientists with computational backgrounds. Since "algorithm predicts everything" remains difficult to establish in the foreseeable future, the impact brought by Claude for Health or Manus is unlikely to manifest primarily in R&D conclusions themselves.

More significant changes may not occur at the "result" level, but in the R&D process—in the question of where human energy is consumed.

Whether Agent or workflow, their core function is not to strip people from thinking, but to extract people from labor that is highly formalizable but has limited value density, allowing finite cognitive resources to re-concentrate on biological problems that cannot yet be formalized but truly determine the direction of innovation—the same applies to other fields. Returning to the historical dimension, "white-collar" and its corresponding work organization method are themselves products of specific technological and institutional conditions: born in the late industrialization phase when information was difficult to standardize, computing costs were high, and processes were hard to replicate; continued in the information age when information gradually automated, computing costs decreased, and processes became replicable; perhaps will perish in the future AI age when information is highly structured, computing costs continue to decrease, and processes can be systematized.

History provides sufficient indication—along with the London calculating clerks, the British Empire's leading advantage in the automobile era also disappeared. The Locomotive Act of 1865, in the name of safety, required self-propelled vehicles to have someone walking ahead carrying a red flag, limiting speed to 4 miles per hour—this institutional arrangement attempting to protect the carriage industry with existing production relations caused Britain to systematically lag behind for decades in the coming automotive industry transformation. People always try to constrain new production relations with old systems, just as today we still try to fix people in established positions with process nodes, job descriptions, and KPI systems to address the uncertainty of new productive forces. The result is often not the elimination of risk, but the dual retention of human and technological potential value.

But this is not a simple "replacement" or "upgrade"—it is a profound "liberation crisis." People liberated from processes have not been prepared with new positions by existing organizations and institutions. When the armor named "professional process" is stripped off, we not only lose the cumbersome, but also lose the shelter. What remains is only that most pure, yet most unfamiliar to modern people, ancient task from Aristotle's perspective: using practical wisdom (Phronesis) to define what problems are truly worth solving.
