Keywords include: AI Agent, transaction cost, complexity allocation, firm boundaries.
Approximately 【3,600】 words | Reading time 【10-12】 minutes

Over the past two years, discussions about whether "firm sizes must shrink in the AI Agent era" have been frequent, yet they often remain superficial. The author previously pointed out in an analysis of organizational forms:

> In an AI-empowered environment, the team's overall value realization ultimately depends on the integration, evaluation, decision-making, and collaborative improvement of cognitive levels for human-machine collaboration outputs. Simply adding more human resources or enhancing AI capabilities will be constrained by the team's overall collaborative efficiency's cognitive boundary limits... The key lies in understanding the abstract structure of the business at its essence and re-examining and dividing organizational forms based on this foundation.
> Alex Su, WeChat Official Account: IntelliPharma Insights
> Breaking the Newcomer Month Effect: How to Build an AI-Native Biopharma Team?

The core of this judgment is not about "how many people or how many AI replacements," but about how complexity is received, digested, and governed in complex systems. Therefore, at the beginning of 2026, re-examining the "big firm vs. small firm" debate is no longer a value judgment on organizational scale, but a more fundamental and explanatory question:

When technology significantly raises the upper limit of complexity a system can handle, who should bear this newly added complexity?

We will set aside for now a realistic but more political economy-flavored premise: how AI Agent platforms' bargaining power escalates with scale effects after forming de facto oligopolies. Under the most basic assumptions of free market pricing and rational organizational choice, the current mainstream views on firm size evolution can be roughly categorized into three types.

(Diagram generated by Nano Banana Pro)

**The Scale-Down School (Most Common)**

> A firm will stop growing (i.e., reach its optimal size) when the marginal cost of organizing an extra transaction internally equals the cost of carrying out the same transaction on the market.
> https://www.charlescoverdale.com/p/coase-vs-ai

This view holds that AI technology reduces market transaction and collaboration costs, enabling firms to outsource complex tasks previously handled internally (such as data analysis, customer service) to specialized AI services or platforms in the market. Complexity is thus "marketized"—firms no longer need to maintain large functional systems internally, naturally leading to smaller and more agile organizational scales.

**The Boundary-Flow School (Increasingly Mature, Corresponding to Baldwin & Clark's《The Power of Modularity》)**

> Instead of asking "Should we build this internally or outsource it?" the question becomes "How can we best orchestrate AI systems to create value, regardless of traditional organizational boundaries?"
> https://medium.com/@apoorvkhanna/the-nature-of-the-ai-driven-firm-rethinking-coases-theory-for-the-digital-age-378ade604c1d

This view emphasizes that AI technology promotes the modularization and standardization of complex tasks. Firms are no longer viewed as stable, closed all-purpose executors, but evolve into temporary, dynamically reconfiguring coordination nodes. Their core capability lies not in owning single resources, but in orchestrating models, tools, people, processes, and AI Agents into an automatically operating value chain. Complexity no longer fixedly belongs to any organization but flows across different organizations, platforms, and agents based on projects and phases, resulting in highly elastic firm boundaries.

**The Scale-Up School (Complexity Elevation)**

Unlike the first two, this view holds that AI truly elevates the upper limit of complexity that can be effectively managed within an organization. When firms face multi-technology coupled, long-cycle, high-uncertainty systemic problems (such as personalized medicine, globalized new retail, or deeply engineered frontier manufacturing), re-"internalizing" key complexity becomes more efficient and strategically meaningful. This leads to significant increasing returns to scale in key factors like data, computing power, and talent, giving top firms inherent incentives for continued expansion.

The author believes these three seemingly opposing views—"Scale-Down," "Boundary-Flow," and "Scale-Up"—are not zero-sum either/or competitions, but natural projections of the same question under different complexity-bearing approaches. Firm size evolution is not directly determined by AI technology itself, but depends on whether the industry's inherent complexity barriers can be effectively split, transferred, or internalized. They all point to a more universal logic:

The reallocation of complexity between firms, markets, and society, and the dynamic trade-offs with organizational costs.

The choice of how complexity is internalized, outsourced, or transferred ultimately leads us to the core proposition of firm theory: the classic discourse on firm boundaries and transaction costs by Ronald Coase and Oliver Williamson.

Ronald Coase, the 1991 Nobel Prize in Economics winner and representative of the Chicago School, proposed the classic framework later summarized as Transaction Cost Theory in 1937. Transaction cost theory explains firm size—firms exist to reduce market transaction costs (such as information search, negotiation and contracting), and their boundaries depend on the marginal equilibrium point between market transaction costs and internal organizational management costs: when internal coordination is cheaper, firms expand; when market transactions are more efficient, firms contract.

This logic is easily understood at the micro level intuitively. Take HR decisions as an example: when the long-term marginal cost of hiring a full-time employee to complete a certain job is lower than the market transaction costs of continuously outsourcing that work (including hidden costs like screening, communication, collaboration friction), this headcount has positive ROI at the organizational level. In businesses with stable operations and mature processes, firm size is in fact the organizational expression of its ability to carry and process task complexity. In the software engineering era, this equivalence is particularly clear: "people + software" absorbs almost all controllable complexity in the system, making employee size an observable indicator of complexity internalization.

Oliver E. Williamson, the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics winner, further advanced transaction cost theory from "whether to make or buy" to "how to govern." He pointed out that transaction costs are not abstract constants, but are jointly determined by three characteristics: asset specificity, uncertainty, and transaction frequency. Different characteristic combinations correspond to different optimal governance structures: low asset specificity corresponds to market governance, medium specificity to hybrid governance, while high asset specificity and high uncertainty endogenously drive transactions into hierarchical internal governance within firms. Under this framework, firms are no longer just "production functions," but a governance technology used to curb opportunism, counteract bounded rationality, and systematically reduce transaction costs.

By breaking down OpenAI's cost structure in 2025, we will see: AI does not simply reduce costs, but reshapes the cost curve of "which complexity should be internalized and which should be outsourced."

- **Inference Costs**: In the first nine months of 2025, OpenAI's inference spending on Microsoft Azure has surged to $8.67 billion, more than double the entire 2024 figure;
- **R&D / Training**: Full-year 2025 R&D spending is expected to be approximately $14.6 billion, mainly consisting of model training costs, data costs, and R&D personnel costs;
- **Staff & Compensation**: In 2025, OpenAI's average stock-based compensation (SBC) per employee reached $1.5 million, with overall personnel and administrative expenses expected to be approximately $5.8 billion;
- **Infrastructure Investment**: Involves large-scale computing and hardware commitments, but specific GPU procurement and depreciation data have not yet been fully disclosed.

These costs can be clearly mapped to different degrees of asset specificity. Computing infrastructure and model training capabilities are clearly highly asset-specific assets: their use is highly dependent on the LLM specific technological paradigm, with almost no cross-purpose secondary market liquidity. Once outsourced, such assets are highly exposed to opportunism risks and coordination failures, naturally converging toward hierarchical governance structures. This explains why after Gemini 3's release, capital markets gave positive feedback to Google's vertical integration capabilities, and why OpenAI subsequently made a series of moves in self-developed hardware and infrastructure—these are rational results of minimizing transaction costs.

In contrast, data acquisition remains in a typical hybrid governance zone. On one hand, "free data" obtained early through public resources like Common Crawl is rapidly depleting; on the other hand, legal and compliance boundaries are not yet fully solidified, allowing third-party data service providers like Bright Data to exist in a gray but tradable market structure. Data thus becomes a key asset whose specificity is rapidly increasing but not yet fully internalized.

Its high costs do not stem from "data scarcity" itself, but from: LLMs cannot achieve effective generalization in domains completely lacking empirical samples, forcing firms to "translate" complexity originally dispersed in the industry system into learnable internal capabilities through paid data. The key question thus is not whether AI is stronger, but: which complexity should continue to be outsourced through the market, and which has rationally entered within firm boundaries. In this sense, the divide between AI platform firms and non-AI product firms is essentially a different choice regarding the "degree of complexity internalization."

(Training dataset costs from https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.12427)

Therefore, from the rational firm perspective of transaction costs, looking back at AI firms' financial structures yields an not pleasing but highly consistent conclusion: if the generation of certain value depends on highly specialized, non-scalable data that can only be continuously supplied by domain experts in the form of "manual labeling," these experts do not have economic necessity to enter AI firms to sell their labor. Instead, this type of complexity should remain in the original industry system, with AI being called upon as a tool, rather than being platform-absorbed.

Returning to the pharmaceutical industry, who has the qualification to internalize business complexity? The answer does not depend on who has larger models, more computing power, or more aggressive capital structures, but on who can take on complexity that cannot be accessed by free internet data and cannot be absorbed by general AI platforms at marginal costs. In key areas such as drug discovery, translational medicine, clinical development, and CMC, having a complex system capability that can collaborate with AI but cannot be replaced by it.

Therefore, what is truly eliminated in the AI era is not SMEs themselves, but organizations that neither own complexity nor can effectively outsource complexity, yet try to "consume complexity" through platformization narratives. Returning to the question at the beginning of the article, the answer also emerges: firms that truly bear complexity must still maintain their hierarchical governance structures and large organizational forms; AI will not dissolve these structures but merely become the "glue" connecting high-complexity systems, and raise higher requirements for how organizations harness complexity.

Tools do not await labor, but "Dao" reaps profits. Truly solid monopolies are often not built on complexity, but on positions where profits can be earned without bearing complexity—AI will serve as the efficiency amplifier for their continued returns and begin to replace the few complexities still borne by humans. And this, perhaps, is the moment when institutions need to be re-questioned.
