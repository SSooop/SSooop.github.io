Over the weekend, a rumor circulated that Eli Lilly might acquire Insilico Medicine. The website of the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) did indeed contain an intriguing entry: [“20261000: Eli Lilly and Company; InSilico Medicine Cayman TopCo.”](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/early-termination-notices/20261000)

The FTC enforces federal antitrust and consumer-protection laws. Under the Hart–Scott–Rodino Antitrust Improvements Act, transactions that meet the reporting conditions must be notified to the FTC and the Department of Justice’s Antitrust Division and generally cannot close before the statutory waiting period ends. Effective February 17, 2026, the most commonly cited minimum size-of-transaction threshold rose to [$133.9 million](https://www.ftc.gov/enforcement/competition-matters/2026/01/new-hsr-thresholds-filing-fees-2026).

The early-termination record named Lilly, Insilico Medicine Cayman TopCo, and Insilico entities associated with intellectual property, Hong Kong operations, and the United States. It is therefore understandable that the market interpreted the filing as a possible acquisition. Yet an HSR record only establishes that a reportable transfer of assets, securities, or rights passed through the relevant antitrust process. On its own, it does not prove that the entire company is being acquired.

On the evening before this article was published, Insilico announced a [global R&D collaboration](https://insilico.com/news/uiy12zcjg1-insilico-medicine-announces-global-rampd%3Famp%3Dtrue) with Lilly. Insilico will receive $115 million upfront and is eligible for development, regulatory, and commercial milestone payments worth up to approximately $2.75 billion in total, plus tiered royalties. Congratulations to Insilico.

More important than the legal structure of the transaction is what it reveals about the value logic of AI-powered drug discovery. In the previous essay, [“Participating in the Evolution of AI + Pharma”](/en/blog/2026/participating-in-ai-pharma-evolution/en), I argued:

> An AI-enabled innovative-drug company remains, at its core, an innovative-drug company. Payment is constrained by national insurance systems and commercial payers. Price pressure in China pushes companies toward global licensing, overseas development, and dollar markets for fuller price discovery. Compute, data, and specialized AI talent further raise upfront costs.

This essay takes that argument one step further.

Much public analysis asks whether a supposedly “AI pipeline” has proved the power of AI, or simply equates AI’s value with a higher probability of success for one asset. That framing remains trapped in the target-centric narrative of conventional pharmaceutical analysis rather than shifting to an AI narrative.

Consider a fast-follower. If tozorakimab reports positive Phase III results in COPD, the market will usually become much less skeptical of the IL-33 target. Generate:Biomedicines’ GB-0895, by contrast, cannot tell us by itself how valuable the company’s generative-biology platform is. One project—successful or unsuccessful—can neither validate nor invalidate an entire platform.

AI as a methodology still requires evidence. But the object of evaluation should not be a single molecule advertised as “AI-discovered.” It should be whether the company achieves systematic improvement across projects and cycles. Does data return to the system? Do models improve experimental selection? Does failure become organizational knowledge? Does the portfolio update faster? If so, the company is demonstrating data infrastructure, systematized R&D, and technical management capable of competing in the next era—not the value of the letters “AI” themselves.

Returns in large pharmaceutical portfolios follow a strongly right-skewed, long-tailed distribution. An L.E.K. analysis of 15 global biopharmaceutical companies examined roughly 600 assets and found that approximately 100 were expected to generate 80% of revenue growth from 2024 to 2030. A small number of blockbuster assets determine most portfolio growth.

From a valuation perspective, AI should therefore not be described simply as lifting the average success rate of every pipeline. Its more important effect may be to change the shape of the return distribution: reducing the cost of exploration, accelerating portfolio renewal, increasing the number of testable hypotheses, and improving a company’s ability to capture events in the right tail.

Pipeline success depends on several interacting dimensions. R&D has the binary-option character of success or failure, while commercialization and asset transfers resemble the distribution of structured assets. Capital markets therefore affect the pricing mechanism differently at each layer:

1. **Scientific and technical validation:** this is closest to a hard constraint. It includes target biology, molecular design, druggability, and the validity of the platform itself. Capital has relatively limited influence; the structure resembles a technical binary option.
2. **Clinical development strategy:** indication selection, endpoint design, and biomarker strategy form the mesoscopic variables of development. They remain scientifically constrained but are also shaped by organizational experience, giving management and capital a moderate influence.
3. **Business development and commercialization:** once R&D becomes asset pricing, licensing, NewCo structures, and multinational commercialization all involve capital structure, risk sharing, and global distribution. Their effect on valuation is often decisive.

The real question for an AI drug discovery company is therefore not whether AI can guarantee that one pipeline succeeds. It is whether AI lets the company remain in this right-tail game with better costs, faster feedback, and a higher-quality portfolio.

AI drug discovery still has a relatively weak presence in the venture market compared with model-layer companies represented by large language models. OECD data show that AI firms raised approximately $258.7 billion in global venture funding in 2025, equal to 61% of all VC investment. By contrast, a study covering 28,269 biopharma financing deals from 2010 to 2024 identified only 1,679 AI-related deals, or 5.93%.

The gap creates a pronounced one-sided sentiment structure. The large-model boom of recent years spilled its narrative premium into biopharma. Typical FOMO—fear of missing out—can be seen both in venture funding and in public-market volatility around AI drug discovery companies.

Sentiment is clearly insufficient. A useful line attributed to Chinese investor Ge Weidong is: “People base their judgments on the two dimensions of facts and data, yet the game they participate in unfolds in the third dimension of emotion and the fourth dimension of dreams.”

Where, then, is the short- and long-term “dream” of AI drug discovery? A four-quadrant framework built around time horizon and technological progress offers a more useful explanation.

- **Short-term optimism:** AI accelerates pipeline generation, expands business-development opportunities for AI drug discovery companies, and may improve candidate quality and success in well-bounded tasks.
- **Short-term pessimism:** AI improves only a small portion of the pipeline-generation process. It does not provide an overwhelming advantage over conventional biotech, and strong evidence that AI has already transformed the clinical success rate of novel targets remains scarce.

An early analysis published in _Drug Discovery Today_ in 2024 reported that AI-discovered molecules entering Phase I had a higher success rate than historical industry benchmarks. Yet the sample was limited, and many projects remained concentrated in known targets, molecular optimization, or established biological space. The result is worth watching, but it cannot settle the question for an entire industry.

- **Long-term optimism:** AI substantially increases the rate and portfolio-level success of pipeline generation while lowering development cost. More radically, new molecular modalities, mechanisms of action, and research paradigms could alter the competitive and pricing structure of pharmaceutical markets.
- **Long-term pessimism:** AI produces diminishing marginal returns. Biological complexity remains irreducible and continues to dominate competition. As AI tools diffuse into industry standards, early advantages are competed away and pipelines return to binary-option pricing near traditional success rates.

At this stage, evaluating the short-term AI-pharma thesis only by asking whether the technology “works” understates the structural force behind it. Most biotech companies are adopting AI to some degree, while pipeline success already depends on many variables. The relative premium of AI drug discovery companies therefore comes partly from a cannot-be-bearish consensus in capital markets.

In a game where systematically betting against AI’s future is difficult, structural preference becomes a source of premium. Investors do not need to believe that every AI drug discovery model will succeed. They need only fear that complete absence from AI may mean missing a reconstruction of the industry’s production function.

At the same time, there is a fundamental mismatch between AI investment and pharmaceutical monetization. Even AI-native drug companies cannot price model capability as directly as large-model software firms can. Software can distribute marginal improvements in model performance to vast numbers of users. Drug companies still monetize primarily through the medicine as an end product, forcing the value of the model through layers of experimentation, clinical development, regulation, reimbursement, and commercialization.

Long-term pessimists take a partly flawed path to a conclusion that is not entirely wrong: complexity does not disappear. Software engineering repeatedly expresses this constraint as “no silver bullet.” In biopharma, complexity remains anchored in the ability to understand and manipulate living systems.

If AI does not systematically change the competitive landscape, then even a large industry-wide improvement in pipeline speed, success, and cost may be offset by faster competition, more comparable programs, and higher market standards. Binary-option pricing does not disappear automatically.

But companies do not operate with equal capability. The transition from internet platforms to the large-model era illustrates the point. Even in familiar settings such as short-video generation or content communities, the depth of management’s understanding of AI and the organization’s ability to deploy it produce large differences in product and commercial outcomes.

AI-native drug discovery companies build their organizational structures, data systems, and decision processes around AI from the beginning. They are therefore more likely to establish an early lead in enterprise-wide AI transformation and subsequent operations. That advantage initially appears as differences in efficiency, cost, and feedback speed. Amplified by capital cycles and market sentiment, it can develop into path dependence in data accumulation, talent density, partnership networks, and asset selection.

The more optimistic long-term case is that accumulated operational advantage eventually turns quantitative change into qualitative change. AI-native organization expands into AI-native molecular modalities, mechanisms, and clinical strategies—and thereby begins to reshape the competitive landscape itself.

Maintain constraints amid uncertainty, and patience amid structural change.  
**Let us choose to be long-term optimists.**
